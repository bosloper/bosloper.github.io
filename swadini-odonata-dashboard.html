Section 1
Annual Overview — Species Richness & Records

Observations were conducted across seven complete field seasons from May 2019 to 27 August 2025. Survey windows varied in duration: 2019 had two separate visit periods (2 May–4 August and 11–18 November); 2020 was shortened by COVID-19 lockdown restrictions to August–October only; 2021 represented the most intensive survey year. All 2025 data is now complete (last record 27 August 2025).

Records from 2019–2024 were originally submitted to the Virtual Museum (VMU) at the University of Cape Town and transferred to Observation.org in 2025 with Accepted (with evidence) validation status. Records from December 2024 onward were uploaded directly to Observation.org. From 2025 the observer's use of the Observation.org mobile app enabled records without photographic evidence — this allowed detection of additional species including Anax imperator, Phyllomacromia picta, and Pantala flavescens that might otherwise have been missed.

Drought & Ecological Context — Late 2024 and 2025
During the late-summer months of 2024 and continuing into 2025, the Swadini area experienced markedly below-average rainfall compared to previous seasons. The observer noted a conspicuous decline in bird and insect numbers across both seasons. Vegetation also transitioned to its winter state earlier than usual and more abruptly — indicating physiological stress consistent with drought conditions. These factors likely contributed to the reduced species richness recorded in 2024 (38 species) and may partially explain the more modest 2025 numbers relative to survey effort.

Section 3
Phenology — Species Richness by IOC Week

In the Southern Hemisphere, the austral summer peak (January–March) falls before the observer's annual arrival at Swadini, which typically begins in late April or early May. By the time surveys commence around IOC weeks 17–18, the post-summer diversity is already declining from its January–April maximum. The survey therefore captures the late-summer descent, the winter trough, and the early warm-season recovery — but not the true peak.

Within the observed window, species richness is highest at the start of each season (weeks 18–22, May) — reflecting the tail of the summer-peak community — and again shows a modest uptick from week 33 onward (August) as temperatures recover after the mid-winter low. The 2019 November survey (weeks 45–47) recorded 38 species in a single month, confirming that the late warm-season period is also highly productive. The August window is substantially under-sampled and is likely more diverse than the records suggest.

Week 33 anomaly — explained
Week 33 (mid-August) shows an unusually high record count (188 total, vs ~90–130 for adjacent weeks). The bulk of this comes from 2022, which contributed 75 of the 188 records in that week, plus 25 species — both the highest single-week record count and species count from any single year in week 33. This appears to reflect particularly good surveying conditions or weather in that specific week in 2022 rather than a genuine ecological phenomenon. The 2021 and 2025 contributions to week 33 were more typical (26 and 42 records respectively).

Species richness weeks 28–35 — late-winter transition by year

This window captures the end of the winter trough and onset of the early warm-season recovery. July typically ends around week 30–31; August runs weeks 31–35. The observer's personal recollection of July being the lowest-activity month is confirmed by the data.

Section 4
Winter Phenology & Core Resident Species

Despite reduced activity in winter (June–August, IOC weeks 23–34), Swadini supports a robust core of species present throughout the cold season. Twelve species were recorded in winter in all 7 seasons, and a further 10 species were present in winter in 5 or 6 seasons, confirming that Odonata diversity at this site remains high year-round.

July (weeks 27–30) is consistently the month of lowest species count — corroborating the observer's field impression. Species showing the steepest July trough tend to be the Libellulidae skimmers and dropwings that prefer warm, sunny perching conditions. The warm-season specialists (e.g. Acisoma spp., Ictinogomphus ferox, Bradinopyga cornuta) essentially disappear from records through July and August, most likely still present as larvae or early adults in sheltered habitat.

Phenological Patterns — Southern Hemisphere Context

True summer peak (Jan–Apr) — not surveyed: The warmest, wettest months at Swadini are January–April. Odonata diversity almost certainly reaches its annual maximum during this period, when water temperatures are highest and most species are simultaneously active as adults. This window is entirely unrepresented in the dataset. The observer's hypothesis that many Orthetrum and other warm-season skimmers would be far more numerous January–March is well-supported by their phenological profile in neighbouring regions.

Early survey window (Apr–May, wks 17–22): The observer arrives to find species already past their peak. Records at this time nonetheless capture 35–42 species — a very high count — representing the diversity still present as summer winds down. Gomphidae (clubtails) tend to be among the earliest to appear in the spring warm-up and are regularly recorded at the start of each season.

August–October — under-sampled opportunity: The 2020 survey (Aug–Oct) recorded 20–37 species per month, and the 2019 November survey produced 38 species. This confirms that the late warm-season recovery is substantial. A recommendation to extend surveys into September–October annually would significantly improve phenological coverage and likely add further species to the site list.

Notable observations

Orthetrum julia normalisation: Records as O. julia falsum in 2025 and as O. julia in previous years have been merged into a single entry. After normalisation this species was recorded in all 7 seasons and is properly classified as a core resident.

Pantala flavescens (Pantala, 2025): Recorded using the Observation.org mobile app — the first record at this site made without photographic evidence. A cosmopolitan migratory species known for inter-continental movements; its appearance reflects a local immigration event. Mobile app usage from 2025 enables detection of fast-flying or distant species not previously captured.

Zygonoides fuelleborni (Southern Riverking, 2019): A range-restricted species near its southern distribution limit in South Africa; a significant record for the Blyde River Canyon.

Trithemis annulata (Violet Dropwing, 2019): Seen regularly at the Blyderivierspoort Dam itself, approximately 100 m higher than the canyon study site. Its single canyon record likely represents a vagrant from dam level. The canyon still holds mysteries.

Orthetrum single-year records: The observer is confident in the identifications. Multiple Orthetrum species being known only from one or two years is consistent with the hypothesis that the unsurveyed January–March summer peak is their period of maximum abundance and detectability.

Anax imperator and Phyllomacromia picta: Several records of both species were only possible from 2025 onward due to the mobile app allowing photo-free submissions. Previous records of these strong-flying species were likely under-counted.

Section 7
Notable & Rare Species — Confirmed at Swadini

Several species recorded in the study area are considered exceptionally significant due to scarcity, restricted range, or scientific novelty. All records below are supported by photographic evidence submitted to the Virtual Museum (UCT) and/or Observation.org. Size data from Tarboton W & Tarboton M, A Guide to the Dragonflies & Damselflies of South Africa.

★ Microgomphus nyassicus — Eastern Scissortail

Family: Gomphidae (Clubtails)  ·  Suborder: Anisoptera (Dragonfly)  ·  Size: 43 mm length · 54 mm wingspan

This small, cryptically-patterned gomphid was known in South Africa from only two historic records before the Swadini survey began: exuviae found in approximately 1968 at Oribi Gorge, KwaZulu-Natal, and larvae recovered from the Komati River, Mpumalanga (near the eSwatini border) in 2018. No living adult had ever been photographed in South Africa.

First living adult for South Africa was photographed on 20 May 2019 at Swadini by Antoinette Snyman (survey team), with the observer photographing another individual three days later at the same locality. The species was absent from records in 2020 (survey began in July, after the peak observation window) but has been recorded consistently every season from 2021 through 2025. Observations are confined to a specific stretch of the Blyde River within the study area.

Its habitat preferences, behaviour and thermal activity range have been studied across the survey years. Most observed individuals are male. Several females have also been recorded, and copulating pairs (in copula) were photographed during both 2021 and 2025 — providing direct evidence of breeding in the canyon. Weekly records cluster around IOC weeks 18–23 (May), consistent with a short warm-season emergence window.

★ Crocothemis divisa — Rock Scarlet

Family: Libellulidae (Skimmers)  ·  Suborder: Anisoptera (Dragonfly)  ·  Size: 36–39 mm length · 60–68 mm wingspan

A seldom-recorded and under-observed species. The diagnostic features are the wing venation pattern and the complete absence of amber colouration at the bases of the hindwings — characters that reliably distinguish it from the superficially similar Crocothemis erythraea (Broad Scarlet).

A single young male was observed and photographed once in 2019 (IOC week 25, late June) on small rocks at the edge of the Blyde River. It has not been recorded in subsequent seasons. Given the confined survey window and the species' apparent preference for exposed riverine rocks, it may well be present during the unsurveyed summer peak (January–March) and/or in sections of the river not routinely covered.

★ Aciagrion gracile — Graceful Slim

Family: Coenagrionidae (Pond damsels)  ·  Suborder: Zygoptera (Damselfly)  ·  Size: 37–39 mm length · 45 mm wingspan

A small to medium-sized damselfly recorded at Swadini in 2021, 2023, and 2024 (three of seven seasons). Observations are concentrated in the forest interior: individuals are found on the moist forest floor in deep shade, hanging from low vegetation or fluttering slowly through mottled sunlight beneath the forest canopy, actively foraging for small insects and plant lice.

A particularly interesting phenological observation concerns colouration: individuals encountered during mid- to late-winter (July–August) are a soft salmon-pink colour. This gradually transitions toward blue as the season advances, and by the time the observer departs at the end of August, the blue phase is beginning to manifest. Friends revisiting the same locality on 12 September 2021 noted that some males already showed substantial blue colouration on the thorax and abdomen. This slow winter development — from pink teneral-like colouration toward adult blue — is indicative of an extended larval-to-adult transition period and a delayed attainment of full sexual maturity over the cooler months.

★ Orthetrum brachiale — Banded Skimmer

Family: Libellulidae (Skimmers)  ·  Suborder: Anisoptera (Dragonfly)  ·  Size: 43–47 mm length · 74–82 mm wingspan

A medium-sized, little-known skimmer that is seldom encountered across its range. At Swadini it was recorded on a single occasion in 2022 (IOC week 21, late May), observed and photographed by two members of the survey team. It has not been recorded before or since. Its confirmed presence at the site demonstrates the value of multi-season surveying: a single sighting by experienced observers with photographic documentation constitutes a meaningful contribution to the species' known distribution in the province.

★ Pseudagrion gamblesi — Great Sprite

Family: Coenagrionidae (Pond damsels)  ·  Suborder: Zygoptera (Damselfly)  ·  Size: 45–50 mm length · 55–65 mm wingspan

A medium-sized sprite of restricted known distribution. Since 2015 this species has been confirmed with photographic evidence (submitted to Virtual Museum and Observation.org) from only 18 of the 25 km² grid blocks on the national distribution map — making each occupied locality significant.

At Swadini, P. gamblesi is a consistent and regularly recorded resident (present in all 7 seasons), found along the Blyde River at sites where overhanging riparian vegetation reaches down close to the water surface. This habitat specificity — shaded riverbank with low-hanging canopy — is typical of the species. The Swadini population is one of the most consistently documented in South Africa and represents an important long-term monitoring resource for this poorly-known sprite.
