# Chapter Twelve: The Union – 1902 to 1910 – The Birth of a Nation

## I. The Ashes

The war was over. The flags of the republics were folded and stored. The Union Jack flew from Pretoria to Bloemfontein.

But the land was ashes.

Thirty thousand Boer farmsteads had been burned. The cattle were gone, the crops were gone, the fences were gone. Families who had trekked across the veld in search of freedom now lived in tents, dependent on British rations, their pride in tatters.

The British called it "reconstruction." The Boers called it "the helot years" – the years of servitude.

They had lost their republics. They had lost their independence. They had lost thousands of their children to typhoid and measles. They had lost everything except their language, their faith, and their memory.

That would be enough.

## II. The Milner Regime (1902-1905)

The British High Commissioner, **Lord Milner**, was determined to remake the conquered republics in Britain's image. He would "Anglicize" the Boers – turn them into English-speaking, loyal subjects of the Empire.

Milner imported thousands of British teachers. He made English the sole language of the schools. He encouraged British immigration. He staffed the civil service with English-speakers. He hoped that, within a generation, the Boer language and identity would be extinct.

He failed.

The Boers resisted not with guns, but with schools. They built their own private Christian-National schools, where children were taught in Dutch and Afrikaans, where the history of the *volk* was honoured, where the suffering of the camps was remembered. The churches – the Dutch Reformed Church – became the heart of the resistance. The *predikant* (minister) was also the teacher, the counsellor, the leader.

Milner's Anglicization campaign drove the Boers together, not apart. A people who had been divided – between *bittereinders* (those who fought to the end) and *handsuppers* (those who surrendered early) – now found common cause in cultural survival.

## III. The Economic Wreck

The war had destroyed the Boer economy. The farms were ruined. The mines had been closed. The towns were depopulated.

The British government offered loans – but the terms were harsh, the interest high, and the bureaucracy intimidating. Many Boers simply gave up. They became *bywoners* – tenant farmers on land that had once been their own. They lived in mud huts, worked for white landlords, and slipped into poverty.

This was the beginning of the **Armblanke Vraagstuk** – the Poor White Problem. Thousands of Afrikaner families, once independent farmers, were now destitute. They drifted to the cities, seeking work, but found themselves competing with Black labour for the lowest wages.

The *armblanke* became a national shame – and a political cause.

## IV. The Political Revival

The Boers had lost their republics, but they had not lost their leaders. **Louis Botha**, the young general who had fought the British to a standstill in Natal, emerged as the political head of the Transvaal Boers. **Jan Smuts**, a brilliant lawyer and philosopher, was his right hand. **J.B.M. Hertzog**, a Free State general with a burning sense of Afrikaner identity, represented the Orange River Colony.

These men had fought the British. Now they would outsmart them.

They founded political parties: **Het Volk** (The People) in the Transvaal, the **Orangia Unie** (Orange Union) in the Free State. They campaigned for self-government, for language rights, for the restoration of Afrikaner dignity.

The British, weary of war and eager for stability, conceded.

In 1906, the Transvaal was granted responsible government. In 1907, the Orange River Colony followed. The Boers, who had surrendered their republics five years earlier, were now governing their own territories.

## V. The Call for Union

Four British colonies now existed in southern Africa: the Cape, Natal, the Transvaal, and the Orange River Colony. They had different laws, different tariffs, different railway gauges. They quarrelled over trade, over customs, over the colour bar.

The British government wanted union – a single, self-governing dominion, like Canada or Australia, that could manage its own affairs and defend its own borders. The Boer leaders also wanted union – but on their own terms.

In 1908, representatives from the four colonies met in **Durban** and then in **Bloemfontein** to draft a constitution. The debates were fierce. The Cape wanted to keep its non-racial franchise (a small number of Black and Coloured men could vote). The Boer republics wanted no Black franchise at all. Natal worried about being dominated by the Transvaal. The Orange Free State worried about being dominated by anyone.

The compromise that emerged was fragile.

## VI. The Union Constitution (1910)

The **South Africa Act** – the constitution of the new Union – was a masterpiece of political engineering. It created:

- A **Parliament** consisting of a House of Assembly (elected) and a Senate (appointed).
- **Executive power** vested in a Prime Minister and Cabinet.
- **English and Dutch** as official languages (Afrikaans would be added in 1925).
- **Three capitals**: Pretoria (administrative), Cape Town (legislative), and Bloemfontein (judicial).

The most contentious issue was the franchise.

The Cape's non-racial franchise was allowed to remain – but only in the Cape. In the other three provinces, only white men could vote. And the Cape's franchise was "entrenched" – it could only be changed by a two-thirds majority of both houses of Parliament sitting together.

It was a compromise that satisfied no one fully – but it allowed the Union to be born.

## VII. The Colour of the Constitution

The Union was a white man's state.

Black South Africans – 70% of the population – were excluded from Parliament, from the Cabinet, from any meaningful voice in their own government. They could vote in the Cape (if they met the property qualification), but that vote was meaningless against the white majority.

The leaders of the new Union – Botha, Smuts, Hertzog – were not cruel men. They were products of their time, their place, their history. They believed – sincerely – that white civilisation was superior, that Black Africans were not ready for self-government, that native policy should be "just but firm."

But their beliefs, however sincerely held, were the foundation of a racial oligarchy. The Union would be a democracy – but only for whites.

## VIII. The Birth of South Africa (31 May 1910)

On 31 May 1910 – the eighth anniversary of the Treaty of Vereeniging – the Union of South Africa came into being.

The celebrations were muted. The war was still fresh. The graves were still raw. But there was hope – a fragile, uncertain hope – that Boer and British could now live together as one nation.

**Louis Botha** became the first Prime Minister. **Jan Smuts** was his deputy. **J.B.M. Hertzog** was a member of the Cabinet – but not for long. The fault lines that would split the Union were already visible.

The Boers had lost their republics. But they had won the peace. They were no longer subjects of the Empire. They were partners – junior partners, perhaps, but partners nonetheless.

The *volk* had survived.

## IX. The Unfinished Business

The Union was a beginning, not an end.

The *armblanke* – the poor white – was not yet lifted. The land of the Black African was not yet secure. The languages of the *volk* – Dutch and Afrikaans – were not yet equal to English. The republic was not yet a republic; the King's head still appeared on the coins, the King's portrait still hung in the courts.

The men who had fought the British would spend the rest of their lives fighting for these things – sometimes through Parliament, sometimes through protest, sometimes through rebellion.

The Union was a house built on sand. It would take another generation to lay the foundations.

But that is a story for the next chapter.
