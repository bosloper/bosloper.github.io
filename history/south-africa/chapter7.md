# Chapter Seven: The Boer Republics – 1845 to 1870 – The Orange Free State and the Transvaal

## I. The Scattering

After the British annexation of Natal, the Voortrekkers scattered across the Highveld like seeds thrown on hard ground. Some went north, crossing the Vaal River into the vast, empty plains beyond. Others stayed south, between the Orange and Vaal Rivers, where the grass was sweet and the water was plentiful.

They did not intend to found nations. They intended to farm, to raise their families, and to be left alone.

But the world would not leave them alone.

Within a generation, two distinct republics had emerged: the **Orange Free State** in the south, and the **South African Republic** (ZAR – *Zuid-Afrikaansche Republiek*) in the north. They shared a language, a faith, and a memory of suffering. But they were different – in character, in governance, and in their relations with the outside world.

## II. The Sand River Convention (1852)

The British, having swallowed Natal, wanted no more trouble from the Boers. They had neither the soldiers nor the will to police the vast interior. So they tried a different approach: recognition.

In 1852, the British representative, **Major William Hogge**, met with Boer leaders north of the Vaal River. The result was the **Sand River Convention**: Britain recognized the independence of the *Zuid-Afrikaansche Republiek* (ZAR) and promised to leave the Boers alone, provided they did not permit slavery.

Two years later, in 1854, Britain signed the **Bloemfontein Convention** with the Boers of the Orange River region. The **Orange Free State** was born – independent, self-governing, and utterly without British oversight.

The Boers had won what they had trekked for: freedom.

## III. The Orange Free State: The Quiet Republic

The Orange Free State was the smaller, poorer, and more stable of the two republics. Its capital was **Bloemfontein** – a sleepy town of wide streets, willows, and the sound of church bells.

The Free State was a farmer's republic. Its citizens lived on isolated farms, growing wheat and maize, raising cattle and sheep. There were no mines, no industry, no great wealth. There was only the land – and the land was good.

Its first president, **Josias Hoffman**, was a decent man but a weak leader. He was followed by **Jacobus Boshof**, who was stronger but quarrelsome. The real architect of the Free State was **Johannes Henricus Brand**, who became president in 1864 and held the office for 24 years.

Brand was a lawyer, a diplomat, and a pragmatist. He kept the Free State neutral – friendly with the British, allied with the Transvaal, but never a pawn of either. Under Brand, the Free State prospered. Its courts were respected. Its treasury was solvent. Its farmers were content.

The Free State was not a nation of heroes or conquerors. It was a nation of farmers – and that, perhaps, was its greatest strength.

## IV. The South African Republic: The Hard Republic

The ZAR was different. It was larger, richer (potentially), and far more difficult to govern. Its capital was **Pretoria**, named after the Voortrekker leader Andries Pretorius. Its first president was Pretorius himself, but he died in 1858, and the republic fell into chaos.

The ZAR had enemies on all sides. To the east lay the **Zulu** kingdom under the great King **Mpande** (and later his son, **Cetshwayo**). To the north lay the **Venda** and the **Pedi**, who fought the Boers for decades. To the west lay the **Tswana** kingdoms, and beyond them, the vast empty spaces of the Kalahari.

The ZAR also had internal enemies. The Boers of the Zoutpansberg district were nearly lawless – smuggling ivory, enslaving children, ignoring the central government. The Boers of Lydenburg and Utrecht often refused to pay taxes or send representatives to the Volksraad.

The ZAR needed a strong leader. It would find one – but not until the 1860s.

## V. The Rise of Marthinus Pretorius

Andries Pretorius's son, **Marthinus Wessel Pretorius**, was determined to unite the Boers. He served as president of the ZAR from 1857 to 1860, and again from 1864 to 1871. He also served as president of the Orange Free State – simultaneously – from 1859 to 1863, trying (and failing) to unite the two republics.

Pretorius was ambitious but not quite capable. He dreamed of a single, unified Boer nation stretching from the Orange River to the Limpopo. But the Boers were too independent, too quarrelsome, too attached to their local freedoms. They would not be united – not yet.

Pretorius did, however, lay the groundwork. He built schools. He organized the commando system. He negotiated with the Zulu and the British. He kept the republic alive.

## VI. The Administration of Justice

Both republics governed themselves through a **Volksraad** (People's Council) – an elected body of white burghers. Laws were based on Roman-Dutch law, brought from the old country. The commandant-general commanded the military; the president governed; the Volksraad made the laws.

In theory, it was a republic. In practice, it was a frontier democracy – loose, informal, and prone to quarrels. Men who disagreed often resorted to shouting, sometimes to fighting, occasionally to shooting.

There were no police – only the *veldkornet* (field-corporal) who organized the commando. There were no jails – only the local landdrost (magistrate) who passed sentence. Justice was swift, simple, and not always fair.

The Boers did not mind. They had not trekked to find justice. They had trekked to find freedom.

## VII. Slavery and Servitude

Both republics prohibited slavery – the British had demanded it as a condition of independence. But the prohibition was a fiction. The Boers had not abandoned their way of life; they had merely renamed it.

Khoisan children captured in commando raids were called *inboekselings* ("inrolled ones") – indentured servants, bound to their masters until adulthood. Black labourers were paid in kind, not cash, and were often forbidden to leave the farm. The distinction between "slave" and "servant" was paper-thin.

The Boers told themselves they were not slaveholders. But the people who worked their fields, tended their cattle, and raised their children knew otherwise.

## VIII. The Beginnings of the Language

On the farms, a new language was taking shape. It was not Dutch – too formal, too foreign. It was not English – too alien, too imperial. It was a kitchen language, a *kombuistaal* – a blend of Dutch, Malay, Portuguese, French, and Khoikhoi words, spoken with a simplified grammar and a distinctive accent.

It was the language of the *volk* – the language of the trekboers, the commandos, the lonely farms under the vast African sky. It had no name yet, and no written form. But it was alive, and it was growing.

One day, it would be called **Afrikaans**.

## IX. The Quiet Years

The 1850s and 1860s were quiet for the Boer republics. There were skirmishes with the Pedi, the Venda, and the Zulu. There were droughts, locust plagues, and cattle diseases. There were quarrels between president and Volksraad, between district and district, between stubborn men who would not yield.

But there was no war with Britain. There was no war with any great power. The Boers farmed, prayed, raised children, and watched the horizon.

They did not know that their quiet years were about to end.

The discovery that would change everything – that would flood the republics with gold, with strangers, with greed, and with war – was just over the horizon.
