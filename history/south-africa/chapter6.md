# Chapter Six: The Great Trek – 1835 to 1845 – The Voortrekkers' Journey

## I. The Gathering Storm

By the mid-1830s, the trekboers of the Eastern Cape had reached a breaking point.

For generations, they had pushed eastward, seeking land and freedom. But the British had followed. The British brought laws the trekboers did not understand – laws that protected the Khoikhoi, that restricted commandos, that made English the language of the courts. The British brought missionaries who preached equality between white and black – an idea the trekboers found not only wrong but blasphemous.

And then, in 1834, the British abolished slavery.

For the trekboers, this was the final insult. They had owned slaves for nearly two centuries. Their farms, their families, their entire way of life depended on unfree labour. The British paid compensation – but the compensation was meagre, the paperwork was in English, and the money had to be collected in London.

The trekboers did not want compensation. They wanted to be left alone.

So they began to talk of leaving – of crossing the great Orange River, of disappearing into the vast, empty interior, of founding a new country where no British flag flew and no British judge sat.

They called it the **Great Trek**.

## II. The Voortrekkers: Who They Were

Not every trekboer joined the Trek. Many stayed, adapted, and eventually made peace with British rule. The Voortrekkers – the "pioneers" – were a minority: the most restless, the most independent, the most unwilling to bend.

They were not poor. A Voortrekker needed resources: oxen, wagons, guns, supplies. They were not desperate. They were determined.

They were also deeply religious. They saw themselves as the new Israelites, fleeing the bondage of Egypt (British rule) for the Promised Land (the interior). They believed God had called them to this journey, and they would not turn back.

## III. The First Waves (1835-1837)

The first Voortrekker groups left the Cape in small, uncoordinated parties. They crossed the Orange River – a broad, brown, sluggish stream – and entered the vast grasslands of the Highveld.

The early pioneers suffered terribly. **Louis Trichardt** and **Hans van Rensburg** led their groups north, toward the Portuguese port of Delagoa Bay (modern Maputo). Van Rensburg's entire party was massacred by Tsonga warriors. Trichardt's group reached the coast, only to be decimated by malaria. Only a handful survived.

But their journals, passed from hand to hand, provided invaluable intelligence: there was land to the north. There were rivers, pastures, and people – some friendly, some hostile. The Trek would continue.

## IV. The Ndebele and the Battle of Vegkop (1836)

The most powerful African kingdom north of the Vaal River was the **Ndebele** under their king, **Mzilikazi**. Mzilikazi had been a general of the great Zulu king Shaka, but had broken away and led his people north, conquering a vast territory that stretched from the Highveld into present-day Zimbabwe.

But Mzilikazi's *impis* (warriors) did not stay within their borders. They raided far and wide – south of the Vaal River, into the grasslands between the Orange and the Vaal – seeking cattle, captives, and tribute.

Mzilikazi saw the Voortrekkers as a threat. He attacked their laagers, drove off their cattle, and killed their stragglers.

In October 1836, a Voortrekker group led by **Andries Potgieter** was attacked at **Vegkop** (near present-day Heilbron, in the Free State – south of the Vaal River) by an Ndebele impi of 5,000 warriors. The 40 Voortrekkers formed a *laager* – a circle of wagons, chained together, with ox-hides hung between to stop spears and arrows. From behind this wall, they fired their muskets.

The Ndebele charged again and again. They could not break the laager. They lost hundreds of men. But they captured almost all the Voortrekkers' cattle – a devastating blow.

The Voortrekkers had won the battle but lost their means of survival. They were forced to retreat, to beg for help from Griqua and Rolong allies, to regroup and try again.

The battle of Vegkop was not a decisive victory. It was a warning: the interior was not empty, and the road north would be paved with blood.

## V. The Natal Route and Piet Retief

Not all Voortrekkers headed north. Some, led by a charismatic farmer named **Piet Retief**, turned east, toward the passes of the **Drakensberg** mountains. Beyond the mountains lay **Natal** – a lush, green land of hills and rivers, with a natural harbour at Port Natal (Durban).

Retief believed Natal could be a new homeland. But first, he needed land.

The land of Natal was controlled by the **Zulu** kingdom under its king, **Dingane**. Dingane had come to power by murdering his half-brother, the great Shaka. He was suspicious, unpredictable, and utterly unwilling to cede territory without assurance.

Retief negotiated. He promised to recover Zulu cattle stolen by a rival chief. He flattered Dingane. He spoke of friendship and trade. Finally, on 4 February 1838, Dingane agreed: if Retief would bring the stolen cattle, he would grant the Voortrekkers land.

Retief brought the cattle. Dingane signed a treaty.

It was a lie.

## VI. The Massacre (6 February 1838)

On the morning of 6 February 1838, Dingane invited Retief and his party to a farewell celebration at his royal kraal, **Mgungundlovu**. Retief brought 70 unarmed men – and 30 young Coloured servants.

The Zulu danced. They sang. And then, without warning, Dingane shouted: *"Bulala abathakathi!"* – "Kill the sorcerers!"

The Zulu fell upon the Voortrekkers, clubbing them to death. Retief was the last to die. His skeleton would be found years later, still holding the treaty Dingane had signed.

The Zulu then swept down on the Voortrekker camps scattered in the foothills of the Drakensberg – at **Bloukrans**, at **Moordspruit**, at **Weenen** (the name means "weeping"). They killed men, women, and children. Nearly 500 Voortrekkers died in a single day.

The Great Trek had become a flight for survival.

## VII. The Winter of Despair

Throughout 1838, the Voortrekkers in Natal clung to the coast, starving, demoralised, and surrounded. Some wanted to give up – to turn back to the Cape, to beg the British for forgiveness.

But a few refused. Among them was **Andries Pretorius**, a wealthy farmer from the Cape who had joined the Trek late. Pretorius was a natural leader: calm, capable, unshakeable. He rallied the survivors.

He also made a vow.

## VIII. The Covenant and Blood River (16 December 1838)

Pretorius gathered 470 men, 64 wagons, and a small cannon. He led them inland, toward Dingane's kraal. On 15 December, he chose a defensive position on the banks of the **Ncome River**.

That night, the Voortrekkers made a covenant with God. If He would grant them victory, they would build a church and forever commemorate the day as a Sabbath.

The next morning, 16 December, the Zulu came.

Ten thousand warriors streamed across the plain, singing their war songs, their spears glinting in the sun. They charged the laager. The Voortrekkers fired. They charged again. The Voortrekkers fired again.

For three hours, the Zulu hurled themselves against the wagons. They could not break through. The laager held. The Voortrekkers' muskets, firing in relays, cut down wave after wave.

When the battle ended, 3,000 Zulu lay dead. The Ncome River was red with blood. Three Voortrekkers had been wounded. None were killed.

The Voortrekkers called it the **Battle of Blood River**.

Dingane fled north and was later killed. The Zulu kingdom was broken – temporarily. The Voortrekkers had won their land.

## IX. The Republic of Natalia (1839-1843)

The Voortrekkers built a town – **Pietermaritzburg**, named after their two fallen leaders, Retief and Maritz. They founded the **Republic of Natalia**, with Pretorius as its commandant-general. They drafted a constitution, elected a Volksraad, and began to farm.

For a few brief years, they had what they wanted: freedom, land, and no British rule.

But the British had other plans.

## X. The British Annexation (1843)

Britain had not forgotten Natal. The harbour at Durban was too valuable to leave in Boer hands. And Britain did not trust the Voortrekkers – their treatment of the Zulu, their embrace of slavery, their contempt for British law.

In 1842, a small British force landed at Durban. After a brief skirmish, the Voortrekkers surrendered. In 1843, Britain formally annexed Natal as a colony.

The Voortrekkers were furious. Most refused to live under the Union Jack. They packed their wagons, crossed the Drakensberg again, and returned to the Highveld – leaving their graveyards and their dreams behind.

The Republic of Natalia was dead.

## XI. The Dispersal

The failure of Natal forced the Voortrekkers to look elsewhere. Some, like **Andries Pretorius**, settled north of the Vaal River, in the land that would become the **South African Republic** (the Transvaal). Others, like **Andries Potgieter**, settled in the region between the Orange and Vaal Rivers – the future **Orange Free State**.

The Great Trek was over. The Voortrekker dream of a single, unified Boer state had failed. But two new republics had been born – and they would survive, against all odds, for another half-century.

## XII. The Legacy

The Great Trek became the founding myth of the Afrikaner nation. It was a story of suffering, courage, and divine deliverance – of a chosen people fleeing oppression, crossing a wilderness, and winning a Promised Land through the grace of God.

The Battle of Blood River was commemorated every 16 December as the **Day of the Covenant** (later the Day of the Vow). It was a sacred day – a day of thanksgiving, of remembrance, of Afrikaner unity.

But the Trek also had a darker legacy. It displaced the Ndebele, crushed the Zulu, and pushed the Xhosa further east. It established the principle that the interior belonged to the Boers – by right of the gun and the covenant.

And it set the stage for the republics, the wars, and the long, bloody struggle between Boer and British that would dominate South African history for the next sixty years.
