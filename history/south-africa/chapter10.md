# Chapter Ten: The Interlude – Gold, Uitlanders, and the Drift to War – 1881 to 1899

## I. The Peace That Was Not Peace

The Pretoria Convention of 1881 restored the ZAR's independence – but not its dignity. Britain retained "suzerainty" over the republic, a vague legal concept that the Boers interpreted as meaningless and the British interpreted as dominion. The British also kept a resident in Pretoria, a watching brief over "native affairs," and the right to veto the ZAR's foreign treaties.

Paul Kruger accepted the terms because he had no choice. But he did not forget them.

The years after Majuba were not peace. They were an armed truce – a pause in which both sides caught their breath, sharpened their weapons, and waited for the next round.

## II. The Discovery That Changed Everything (1886)

Then came the gold.

The Witwatersrand reef was not like other gold deposits. It was not a riverbed to be panned or a hillside to be scraped. It was a vast underground layer of gold-bearing conglomerate, stretching for dozens of miles, buried beneath hundreds of feet of hard rock.

Mining it required capital – enormous capital. It required shafts, headgears, stamp mills, cyanide tanks, and a workforce of thousands. It required engineers, financiers, lawyers, and managers. It required a city.

Johannesburg rose from the veld in a single year. By 1890, it was a sprawling, chaotic, violent metropolis – the largest city in southern Africa, and the richest.

The gold brought money. The money brought *uitlanders*. The *uitlanders* brought the British flag – not physically, but spiritually. Johannesburg was not a Boer town. It was an English town, built on Boer soil, and its inhabitants wanted English laws, English courts, English liberties.

Kruger looked at Johannesburg and saw a cancer growing in his republic's belly.

## III. The Franchise Question

The heart of the conflict was the vote.

The ZAR's constitution granted citizenship only to white men who had lived in the republic for fourteen years, owned property, and swore allegiance to the state. Most *uitlanders* could not meet these requirements – and had no desire to. They were sojourners, not settlers. They came to make their fortunes and return to London, to Melbourne, to San Francisco.

But they wanted a voice in how their taxes were spent. They wanted representation without assimilation. They wanted the benefits of citizenship without the burden of becoming Boers.

Kruger refused. He knew that if the *uitlanders* got the vote, they would vote for annexation. The ZAR would become a British colony without a single shot being fired.

The British government, pushed by the mine owners and the imperialists, took up the *uitlanders'* cause. They demanded reform – a shorter residency requirement, direct representation in the Volksraad, English as an official language.

Kruger offered compromise: seven years, then five, then three – but always with the condition that the *uitlanders* must abandon their foreign allegiances and become citizens of the ZAR. The British rejected every offer.

They did not want a compromise. They wanted a war.

## IV. The Railway War

Kruger also fought the British with steel and steam.

The ZAR's natural export route for gold was through the British ports of Cape Town, Port Elizabeth, and Durban. But British-controlled railways charged high tariffs, and British customs officials inspected every shipment. Kruger resented this dependence.

He built his own railway – eastward, across the Lowveld, to the Portuguese port of Delagoa Bay (modern Maputo). The line was completed in 1894, and the ZAR finally had a route to the sea that bypassed British territory.

Kruger then raised tariffs on goods shipped through British ports, forcing the mines to use the Delagoa Bay line. The British fumed. They called it economic warfare.

Kruger called it survival.

## V. The Jameson Raid (1895-1896)

The raid changed everything.

The idea was Cecil Rhodes's. He planned a coup: the *uitlanders* of Johannesburg would rise in rebellion, while a small army under his friend Leander Starr Jameson would cross the border from Bechuanaland to support them. The ZAR would fall; the British flag would rise.

The uprising never happened. The Johannesburg *uitlanders*, who had plenty of grievances but no appetite for a shooting war, stayed home.

Jameson crossed anyway. His 600 men rode into the ZAR, expecting to be welcomed as liberators. Instead, they were surrounded by Boer commandos and forced to surrender at Doornkop on 2 January 1896.

Kruger was magnanimous. He handed Jameson and his officers over to the British authorities for trial. In London, they were given light sentences and treated as heroes.

The raid was a fiasco. But its consequences were profound.

First, it convinced Kruger that the British would stop at nothing to destroy his republic. He accelerated his arms purchases and fortified the borders.

Second, it radicalised the *uitlanders*. The raid had failed, but the grievances remained. Many began to see armed rebellion as the only path to reform.

Third, it poisoned relations between the Boers and the British. The trust that had existed – however thin – evaporated.

The war that everyone feared now seemed inevitable.

## VI. The German Telegram

On 3 January 1896, Kaiser Wilhelm II of Germany sent a telegram to Paul Kruger. It congratulated the ZAR on defeating the Jameson Raid "without appealing to the help of friendly powers."

The telegram was intended as a gesture of support – and a warning to Britain that Germany had interests in southern Africa. It had the opposite effect. Britain erupted in outrage. The "Kruger Telegram" was seen as a direct challenge to British paramountcy. Anglo-German relations, already strained, deteriorated sharply.

For Kruger, the telegram was a double-edged sword. It showed that European powers might support the ZAR – but it also made Britain more determined than ever to crush the republic before Germany could intervene.

## VII. The Bloemfontein Conference (May-June 1899)

By 1899, the situation was desperate. The *uitlander* population of the ZAR now outnumbered the Boers. Johannesburg was a tinderbox. British troops were gathering on the borders – in Natal, in the Cape, in Bechuanaland.

Kruger agreed to meet the British High Commissioner, **Sir Alfred Milner**, in Bloemfontein. Milner was an imperialist of the old school – he believed that the British Empire was a force for civilisation, and that the Boer republics were an obstacle to progress.

The conference was a failure from the start. Milner demanded immediate citizenship for *uitlanders* who had lived in the ZAR for five years. Kruger offered seven – and then five, with a guarantee that the new citizens would not be used to outvote the Boers. Milner rejected the offer.

Kruger later wrote: *"Milner did not come to Bloemfontein to negotiate. He came to demand. He wanted war."*

## VIII. The Ultimatum (9 October 1899)

On 9 October 1899, the ZAR and the Orange Free State issued a joint ultimatum to the British government. It demanded that British troops be withdrawn from the borders of the republics, and that all disputes be settled by arbitration.

The ultimatum was deliberately provocative. Kruger knew that Britain would not comply. But he wanted the world to see that the Boers had tried every peaceful avenue – and that the British had forced them into war.

The British rejected the ultimatum. On 11 October 1899, the Boer commandos crossed the borders.

The Second Freedom War had begun.

## IX. The Bridge

The first war had been a skirmish – a few weeks of fighting, a handful of battles, a humiliated Britain and a triumphant ZAR.

The second war would be something else entirely. It would last three years. It would consume the republics, scorch the earth, and kill tens of thousands. It would leave behind concentration camps, burnt farms, and a bitterness that would last for generations.

The gold made it inevitable. The Jameson Raid made it personal. The Bloemfontein Conference made it final.

Now the guns would speak.
