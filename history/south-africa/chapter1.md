# Chapter One: The First Stewards – The Khoisan and the Land Before Memory (Before 1652)

## I. The Deep Time

Long before the first *vryburger* planted a furrow in the shadow of Table Mountain, the land was already old. It had seen continents shift and ice ages come and go. It had heard the roar of great beasts that no human eye ever saw.

And then, perhaps 20,000 years ago – perhaps longer – the first people came.

They walked lightly. They left no cities, no temples, no written words. What they left were paintings on rock faces, tools knapped from stone, and the faintest trace of their bones in sandy soil. They were the **San**, and for millennia, they were the only people in southern Africa.

## II. The San: Masters of the Thin Line

If you had met a San hunter in the year 1500, you might have underestimated him. He was small of stature, light in frame. He carried no shield, no spear of iron. His bow was a slender curve of wood, his arrows tipped with bone and coated in a dark, sticky paste.

That paste was death.

The San had discovered something the rest of the world would only learn centuries later: how to turn the larva of a beetle into a neurotoxin so potent that a single scratch could bring down a giraffe, an eland, or a lion. They did not hunt with brute force. They hunted with patience, tracking, and poison.

But hunting was only half their life. The other half was gathering: roots, tubers, berries, melons, the nutritious kernel of the mongongo nut. They knew the names of hundreds of plants – which ones healed, which ones fed, which ones killed. Their knowledge of the veld was encyclopedic, passed down through stories and songs, from grandmother to granddaughter, from hunter to son.

They lived in small bands, rarely more than thirty or forty people. Leadership was fluid, not inherited. A good hunter had influence, not power. A healer – a shaman, called a *!gi:ten* – was respected, but could not command. Decisions were made by consensus, often after long, patient conversations around the fire.

And the fire was sacred.

## III. The Dance of the Eland

The San had no priests, no churches, no scriptures. Their religion was not a set of beliefs but a practice: the **trance dance**. By night, around a blazing fire, the women would clap and sing a rhythmic, hypnotic melody. The men would dance – stamping, sweating, breathing in great heaves – until the *n/om* (spiritual energy) rose in their bodies like a serpent uncoiling.

In trance, the shaman would bleed from the nose. He would fall to the ground, convulsing. He would journey into the spirit world to heal the sick, to chase away evil, to bring rain. And when he returned, he would paint.

The rock art of the San is not decoration. It is not "primitive" doodling. It is a map of the invisible world. Look closely at the caves of the Drakensberg or the Cederberg: you will see eland – magnificent, pregnant, bleeding from the nose. The eland was the most powerful animal, filled with *n/om*. To paint an eland was to capture its power. To enter the dance was to become the eland.

This was their world: intimate, perilous, alive with spirit. They owned nothing and wanted nothing. They were, in the truest sense, the first people.

And they were not alone.

## IV. The Khoikhoi: People of the Cattle

Sometime around 2,000 years ago – the dates are debated, the evidence incomplete – a new people appeared in the southwest. They spoke a language that clicked like the San's, but their way of life was different. They had **cattle**. Fat-tailed sheep. Goats. And with livestock came a new kind of society.

The Europeans would call them *Hottentots* – a word mimicking their speech, meant to demean. They called themselves **Khoikhoi**, meaning "men of men" or "the real people."

Where the San were egalitarian, the Khoikhoi had chiefs. Where the San moved with the seasons, the Khoikhoi moved with their herds – transhumance, the scholars call it – but they had permanent camps, sacred sites, and territories defended against rivals. Cattle were wealth, status, bride price, and connection to the ancestors. A man without cattle was nobody.

The Khoikhoi did not farm in the European sense. The Cape's winter rainfall regime was not suited to millet or sorghum. Instead, they grazed their animals on the lush pastures of the coastal plain, moving inland as the seasons turned. They made pottery – beautiful, thick-walled vessels for storing milk and water. They worked copper, traded for iron, and wore ornaments of shell and bone.

And they met the San not as conquerors but as neighbours. They traded, sometimes clashed, and often intermarried. The lines between "San" and "Khoikhoi" were never as sharp as later anthropologists liked to draw them. Together, they are the **Khoisan** – a deep-rooted branch of the human family.

## V. The First Strangers from the Sea

The first Europeans to reach the Cape came not to stay, but to pass.

**Bartolomeu Dias** rounded the Cape of Storms in 1488, but his crew, terrified and exhausted, forced him to turn back. He named it, prophetically, the Cape of Storms. His king, João II, renamed it the Cape of Good Hope – a name that spoke of possibility, not danger.

**Vasco da Gama** came next, in 1497. He sailed past the Cape, touched at St. Helena Bay, and continued east into the Indian Ocean. He had no interest in the land or its people. He wanted India.

But the sailors who followed him – Portuguese, Dutch, English, French – needed water. They needed meat. They needed to restock their ships after months at sea. And so they came ashore, cautiously, weapons ready, to meet the Khoikhoi.

The encounters were often friendly at first. A Khoikhoi headman would bring cattle to the beach. A European captain would offer beads, copper, old iron. They would barter, drink, and part ways. But the sailors, ignorant of Khoikhoi customs, often offended. They demanded more than was offered. They took without asking. And when a Khoikhoi warrior tried to stop them, violence followed.

By the early 1600s, the reputation of the Cape was dark. Ships stopped there only when necessary. The Khoikhoi, for their part, had learned to be wary of the pale men from the sea. They sometimes refused to trade at all. They drove off landing parties with stones and spears.

It was an impasse.

## VI. The Shipwreck That Changed Everything

In 1647, the Dutch ship *Nieuwe Haarlem* ran aground in Table Bay. Sixty sailors were stranded. They had no choice but to build a shelter, plant vegetables, and wait for rescue.

They waited for nearly a year.

During that time, they learned. They learned which soil grew best, which springs ran sweetest, how to trade with the Khoikhoi without causing a fight. They built a small fort – a "sand fort," they called it – and a garden that fed passing Dutch ships.

When they were finally rescued, their report to the **Dutch East India Company (VOC)** was clear: *The Cape is good. It can support a settlement. If we do not claim it, the English will.*

The VOC listened. It was a business, not a government. Its only interest was profit. But it understood strategy. The Cape was halfway to Java. A refreshment station there would save thousands of lives and millions of guilders.

And so, on the 6th of April 1652, Jan van Riebeeck stepped ashore with three ships and a handful of men.

The Khoikhoi watched from the hills.

They did not know that their world was about to end.

## VII. A Note on Naming

We have used the terms *San* and *Khoikhoi* because they are the most widely recognised today. But we should remember that these are labels imposed by outsiders – by Europeans, by later African groups, by anthropologists. The people themselves had many names, many clans, many loyalties. They were //Xegwi, !Kung, G/wi, Nama, Korana, Griqua. They were not a single "tribe" or "race."

They were human beings, with loves and hates, hopes and fears, no different from us.

Their descendants still live in South Africa – in the Richtersveld, in the Kalahari, in the townships of the Cape Flats. They speak Afrikaans now, or English, or a remnant of their old clicking tongues. They farm, drive taxis, study at universities. They remember, in ways both faint and fierce, that they were here first.

And the land remembers too.
