# Chapter Fifteen: The Great Depression and the Purified Nation – 1924 to 1939 – The Crisis That Forged Apartheid

## I. The House of Hertzog

By the mid-1920s, J.B.M. Hertzog was the master of South African politics. His Pact government had survived. His Civilised Labour Policy had lifted thousands of *armblanke* families. His flag and anthem had given the *volk* symbols of nationhood. He had even persuaded the British Parliament to pass the **Statute of Westminster** (1931), which made South Africa a fully sovereign nation within the British Commonwealth – no longer a dominion, but a partner.

Hertzog had achieved what Botha and Smuts had thought impossible: Afrikaner power, within the Empire, but independent of British control.

But the house of Hertzog was built on sand.

The Great Depression, which began with the Wall Street crash of 1929, swept across the world. In South Africa, it brought drought, unemployment, and despair. The *armblanke*, who had only just begun to climb out of poverty, fell back into it. The cities filled with homeless families. The countryside dried up and blew away.

Hertzog did not know what to do. He had built his career on blaming the British, the Blacks, the Jews. He could not blame the weather.

The *volk* grew restless.

## II. The Fusion (1934)

Hertzog made a fateful decision: he would unite with his old enemy, Jan Smuts, to fight the Depression.

In 1934, the National Party and the South African Party merged to form the **United Party** (UP). Hertzog became Prime Minister; Smuts became his deputy. The "Fusion" was supposed to end the ethnic conflict, to create a single white party for a single white nation.

It was a betrayal.

A group of hardline Afrikaner nationalists – led by **Dr. D.F. Malan**, a Dutch Reformed minister from the Cape – refused to join the Fusion. They walked out of the NP and founded the **Purified National Party** (*Gesuiwerde Nasionale Party*). They called Hertzog a sell-out, a collaborator, a traitor to the *volk*.

Malan's party was small – at first. But it was pure. It rejected the Empire, rejected the Union Jack, rejected any cooperation with English-speakers. It demanded a republic – a free, independent, Afrikaner republic, with no ties to Britain, no nods to the Crown, no compromises.

The *volk* watched. The *volk* waited.

## III. The Broederbond

Behind the Purified National Party stood a secret organisation: the **Afrikaner Broederbond** (Brotherhood).

The Broederbond had been founded in 1918, in a Johannesburg basement, by a group of young Afrikaner intellectuals. Its purpose was to preserve and promote Afrikaner culture, language, and identity. But it quickly became something more: a secret society, a network of influence, a shadow government.

The Broederbond had members in the churches, the universities, the civil service, the police, the military. They met in secret. They swore oaths. They advanced their own – Afrikaners, Calvinists, nationalists – into positions of power.

Malan was a Broederbond member. Many of his followers were. When Hertzog betrayed the NP, the Broederbond shifted its allegiance to Malan.

The Purified National Party was not just a political party. It was a movement – a movement with deep roots, hidden connections, and a long memory.

## IV. The Great Drought (1930-1933)

While the politicians manoeuvred, the land suffered.

The drought of the early 1930s was the worst in living memory. The maize failed. The cattle died. The dams went dry. The dust blew.

Thousands of Afrikaner families abandoned their farms and trekked to the cities – not by choice, but by desperation. They arrived with nothing – no money, no skills, no hope. They settled in shantytowns on the edges of Johannesburg, Pretoria, Bloemfontein. They lived in tin shacks, in cardboard boxes, in the open air.

The *armblanke* problem, which Hertzog had promised to solve, was now worse than ever.

The state responded with public works – roads, railways, irrigation schemes. The most famous was the **Kakamas irrigation project** in the Northern Cape, where poor white families were given land and water in exchange for labour. Other projects followed: the **Hartbeespoort Dam**, the **Vaal Dam**, the **Great Fish River tunnel**.

These projects were not just about water. They were about dignity. An *armblanke* who built a dam was not a beggar. He was a nation-builder. He was restoring the land, restoring his pride, restoring the *volk*.

But the public works could not employ everyone. The *armblanke* remained a wound that would not heal.

## V. The Rise of Malan

Hertzog's Fusion government staggered from crisis to crisis. The Depression eased, but the political divisions deepened.

Malan's Purified National Party grew slowly, steadily, persistently. It won by-elections. It gained seats in Parliament. It attracted the young, the idealistic, the angry – the Afrikaners who had been born after the war, who had no memory of the republics, but who felt the shame of the *armblanke* and the humiliation of British rule.

Malan was not a charismatic speaker like Hertzog. He was dry, intellectual, even cold. But he was disciplined. He was patient. And he was pure.

He never compromised. He never apologised. He never wavered from the goal: a republic, free from Britain, ruled by Afrikaners, for Afrikaners.

The *volk* began to listen.

## VI. The Centenary of the Great Trek (1938)

The moment that transformed Malan's movement from a fringe party into a mass force came in 1938.

That year marked the 100th anniversary of the Battle of Blood River – the centenary of the Great Trek. The NP organised a celebration unlike anything South Africa had ever seen.

Nine ox-wagons, replicas of the Voortrekker wagons, set out from Cape Town and travelled north, following the route of the original Trekkers. They crossed the mountains, forded the rivers, and camped on the veld. Thousands of Afrikaners lined the route – waving flags, singing songs, weeping.

At every stop, speeches were made. At every stop, the story was told: the story of suffering, of sacrifice, of divine deliverance. The story of the covenant, the story of Blood River, the story of the *volk* chosen by God.

The centenary was not a history lesson. It was a religious revival – a pilgrimage, a consecration, a rebirth.

Malan spoke at the final rally in Pretoria, before 100,000 people. He did not speak of politics. He spoke of destiny. He said that the Afrikaner people had survived the British, survived the war, survived the camps – and would now rise again.

The *volk* roared.

The flame was now a fire.

## VII. The Ox-Wagon as Symbol

The ox-wagon became the symbol of the new nationalism – the *Ossewa*.

It represented the Voortrekker spirit – the courage, the faith, the determination to carve a nation out of a wilderness. It represented the *volk* on the move – leaving behind the corruption of the cities, the oppression of the British, the pollution of the Black townships. It represented a return to the land, to the past, to the purity of the frontier.

The *Ossewa* would soon give its name to a new movement – the **Ossewa-Brandwag** (Ox-wagon Sentinel) – a paramilitary organisation dedicated to protecting the *volk* from its enemies.

But that story belongs to the next chapter.

## VIII. The Ghost of the Republic

By 1939, the political landscape had shifted.

Hertzog was old, tired, and losing control. Smuts was waiting, patient and ambitious. Malan was growing, his Purified National Party attracting more and more Afrikaners who had once supported the Fusion.

The *volk* was no longer satisfied with symbols. They wanted substance. They wanted a republic.

The question was not *if* South Africa would leave the British Empire. The question was *when* – and *who* would lead the way.

The war clouds were gathering over Europe. Soon, South Africa would face its greatest test since 1902.

And the *volk* would have to choose: the Empire, or the republic.
