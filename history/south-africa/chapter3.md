# Chapter Three: The Trekboer Frontier – 1700 to 1770 – The Slow Boil

## I. The Squeeze Behind the Mountains

By the dawn of the 18th century, the Cape Peninsula and its immediate hinterland were filling up. The best land along the Liesbeeck, the Eerste River, and the Berg River had been granted, planted, and fenced. The VOC, ever cautious, tried to limit further expansion. They wanted the settlers close, controllable, taxable.

But the settlers had other ideas.

Younger sons of vryburgers could not inherit land. There was no industry, no towns, no jobs. Their choices were few: become a soldier, hire out as a labourer, or *trek* – move eastward, over the mountains, into the vast, empty (to European eyes) plains beyond.

The trekboers chose the third path.

## II. The Life of the Trekboer

A trekboer was not a farmer in the European sense. He did not live in a neat village or ride out to a fenced field. He lived in a **wagon** – a great, creaking ark of wood and canvas, drawn by sixteen oxen, loaded with his family, his bed, his Bible, his gunpowder, and his coffee pot.

He owned **cattle and sheep**. He moved with the seasons: to the mountains in summer for pasture, to the lowlands in winter for shelter. He had no fixed home, only a series of *stad* (temporary camps) where his wife would plant a small garden of pumpkins and melons.

His house was the landscape. His church was a gathering of wagons on a Sunday morning, a traveling *predikant* (if one could be found) reading from the Book of Psalms. His school was his wife's voice, teaching the children to spell from the Bible.

And his enemy was everything that moved – lions, leopards, hyenas that snatched calves from the herd, San hunters who took sheep when hunger bit, rival trekboers who quarreled over water and grazing.

He was, above all, **alone**. A trekboer might ride for days without seeing another white face. His neighbours were the Khoikhoi herders who worked for him (or beside him), and the San who watched him from the hills.

## III. The Gun and the Bible

Every trekboer carried two things: a **roer** (a long, heavy musket) and a **Bybel** (the Dutch Statenvertaling, carried from the old country).

The gun was for hunting and for war. The trekboer lived by his ability to shoot. He provided meat for his family, protection for his herd, and a reputation that travelled faster than he did. A man who could hit a buck at two hundred paces was respected; a man who could not was pitied.

The Bible was for meaning. In the vast, indifferent silence of the Karoo, a man needed to know that his life mattered, that his suffering had purpose, that God had led him to this place for a reason. The Bible gave him that. It told him that he was one of the elect, a child of the Covenant, a new Israel in a new Canaan.

And like the Israelites of old, he was surrounded by enemies – the *heiden* (heathen) who did not know God, who did not honour property, who threatened his children.

This fusion of gun and Bible – of self-reliance and divine election – would become the core of the Afrikaner identity. It was forged not in churches or schools, but in the dust of the frontier.

## IV. The Khoisan on the Run

As the trekboers pushed eastward, they encountered the remnants of the Khoisan – the same people who had once herded cattle on the Cape peninsula. Now they were scattered, decimated by smallpox, displaced by war, clinging to the arid margins of the colony.

Some Khoisan worked for the trekboers as shepherds and labourers. They were called *knechten* (servants) or *burgers* (a term that disguised their real status). They lived in the wagons, ate from the campfire, and learned to speak Dutch – a rough, broken Dutch that would eventually flavour the new language of Afrikaans.

Others resisted. They raided the trekboers' herds, drove off cattle, killed sheep. The trekboers responded with **commandos** – armed posses of neighbours who tracked the raiders, shot them, and sometimes sold their children into servitude.

There was no law out here. The VOC's jurisdiction ended at the mountains. Justice was what a trekboer could enforce with his own gun and his neighbour's help.

The Khoisan learned to fear the trekboer's lash. But they also learned his language, his God, his ways. Their descendants would become the **Basters** and **Griqua** – mixed communities who would later play their own role in the history of the interior.

## V. The San: The Shadow in the Hills

The San – the *Bosjemans*, the Europeans called them – were different. They would not work for the trekboers. They would not submit. They were hunters, not herders, and they saw the trekboers' cattle as simply another source of meat.

Conflict was inevitable. The trekboers, who needed their herds to survive, could not tolerate the loss of even a few beasts. They organized commandos to track the San, to kill the men, to capture the women and children as *inboekselings* (indentured labourers).

The San fought back with poisoned arrows, with stealth, with a knowledge of the terrain that no white man could match. But they could not win. The guns of the trekboers were longer-ranged, more lethal. The trekboers could replace a dead man from the Cape; the San could not replace a dead hunter.

By 1770, the San of the southwestern Cape had been broken. The survivors fled deeper into the interior – into the Karoo, into the Kalahari – where they would remain, hunted and haunted, for another century.

The trekboers had established a principle: *this land is ours, by right of the gun and the Bible.* It was a principle they would soon extend to the black African kingdoms beyond the Fish River.

## VI. The Legal Vacuum and the Rise of the Kommando

The VOC, in Amsterdam, viewed the trekboers with suspicion. They were tax-evaders, troublemakers, a drain on the Company's resources. But the VOC could not stop them. There were too many, the frontier was too vast, and the officials at the Cape had their own problems.

In practice, the trekboers governed themselves. When a crime was committed – a cattle raid, a murder, a dispute over a waterhole – the local men gathered, elected a *veld-kornet* (field-corporal) or *kommandant*, and rode out to settle the matter.

This was the **kommando system**: a citizen militia, self-armed, self-organized, and answerable only to its own sense of justice. It was rough, arbitrary, and often brutal. But it worked – after a fashion.

The kommando became the template for later Afrikaner resistance. When the British tried to impose their own law on the frontier, the trekboers remembered that they already had a law – the law of the laager, the law of the gun.

## VII. Cultural Seeds: Language, Dress, and Food

**Language:** The Dutch of the trekboers was changing. It lost its formal inflections. It borrowed words from Khoikhoi (*kierie* for walking stick, *dagga* for marijuana), from Malay (*baie* for very, *piesang* for banana), from French (*wa* for wagon). It simplified its grammar: *heb jij* became *het jy*; *ik heb* became *ek het*. It was becoming a *kombuistaal* – a kitchen language – that would one day be called Afrikaans.

**Dress:** The trekboer wore *kleren* made from rough cloth, wide-brimmed hats against the sun, and *veldskoene* (leather boots) that could survive the thorns. His wife wore simple dresses, a *doek* (headscarf), and an apron. Both wore *kappies* (bonnets) against the dust.

**Food:** They ate *biltong* (dried meat), *boerewors* (spiced sausage), *mieliepap* (maize porridge), and *roosterkoek* (griddle bread). They drank *koffie* (coffee), *tee* (tea), and a rough brandy distilled from grapes. They preserved meat with salt and fat, and vegetables with vinegar.

These were not luxuries. They were necessities – the products of a life that could not rely on shops or supply ships.

## VIII. The First Slavery in the Interior

Not all the labour on the frontier was Khoisan. The trekboers also owned slaves – men and women brought from Madagascar, from Mozambique, from the East Indies. These slaves lived in the wagons, worked the small fields, and served as domestic servants.

Their lives were brutal. They could be bought, sold, inherited, and beaten at will. The trekboers saw no contradiction between their Calvinist piety and their ownership of human beings. The Bible, they believed, sanctioned slavery. The children of Ham were cursed to serve the children of Shem.

The children of Ham, in the trekboers' reading, included all the dark-skinned peoples of Africa.

## IX. The Frontier Creeps Eastward: The First Encounters with the Xhosa

By the 1760s, the vanguard of the trekboers had reached the **Fish River** – a sluggish, reed-choked stream that marked the traditional boundary between the Cape's winter rainfall and the summer rainfall of the east.

Beyond the Fish River lay the land of the **Xhosa**.

The Xhosa were not a single tribe but a cluster of chiefdoms – the Gcaleka, the Rharhabe, the Ndlambe – all speaking a Bantu language, all herding cattle, all living in settled villages with chiefs and councils. They had iron, they had spears, they had a military tradition that had repelled the Portuguese two centuries earlier.

The trekboers did not know this. They saw only empty (to them) pastures, and beyond them, the blue hills of the **Zuurveld**. They pushed their herds across the Fish River, seeking new grazing.

The Xhosa noticed.

## X. The End of the Slow Boil

By 1770, the frontier was not a line but a zone – a hundred miles of contested ground where Khoisan, trekboer, and Xhosa met, traded, fought, and occasionally married. The VOC had lost control. The trekboers were beyond reach. The Xhosa were growing alarmed.

The slow boil was about to become a rolling boil. The **Xhosa Wars** – a century of intermittent warfare – were just over the horizon.
