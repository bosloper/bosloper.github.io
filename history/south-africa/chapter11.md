# Chapter Eleven: The Second Freedom War – 1899 to 1902 – The Struggle for Existence

## I. The Ultimatum

On 9 October 1899, the South African Republic and the Orange Free State issued a joint ultimatum to the British government. It demanded that British troops be withdrawn from the borders of the republics, and that all disputes be settled by arbitration.

The British government rejected the ultimatum. The deadline passed. On 11 October 1899, the Boer commandos crossed the borders into British territory.

The Second Freedom War had begun.

Paul Kruger did not want war. He knew the ZAR could not defeat the British Empire in a long conflict. But he believed – he had to believe – that the Boers could win a short one. They had done it before, at Majuba in 1881. Majuba was eighteen years ago. The men who had fought there were now generals, fathers, grandfathers. They remembered the smell of the smoke, the crack of the rifles, the flight of the British down the hill.

They believed God would give them victory again.

## II. The First Phase: The Boer Offensive (October – December 1899)

The Boers struck first. They invaded Natal and the Cape Colony, besieging the British garrisons at **Ladysmith, Kimberley, and Mafeking**. They hoped to force a quick peace before British reinforcements could arrive.

The sieges were the first shock. At **Ladysmith**, 13,000 British soldiers and civilians were trapped for 118 days. At **Kimberley**, Cecil Rhodes himself was inside the town, sending frantic telegrams to London. At **Mafeking**, a young colonel named **Robert Baden-Powell** held out against a much larger Boer force, becoming a hero to the British public.

But the Boers could not take the towns. They had the numbers, the guns, and the courage – but they did not have the artillery to breach modern defences. The sieges dragged on.

## III. Black Week (December 1899)

The British attempted to relieve the sieges. They failed – spectacularly.

On 10 December, at **Stormberg** in the Cape, a British column stumbled into a Boer ambush and was routed. On 11 December, at **Magersfontein**, the Highland Brigade marched into the sights of Boer marksmen hidden in trenches at the foot of a hill. **Koos de la Rey**, the Boer general, had invented a new tactic: don't defend the top of the hill, defend the bottom. The Highlanders were cut to pieces. On 15 December, at **Colenso** in Natal, **Louis Botha** repulsed a British attack with devastating rifle fire.

Britain called it **"Black Week."** Three defeats in five days. The empire was stunned. The Boers celebrated. But they could not follow up their victories. They had no reserves, no supply lines, no plan for a long war.

## IV. The Second Phase: The British Counter-Offensive (January – September 1900)

The British sent a new commander: **Lord Roberts**, with **Lord Kitchener** as his chief of staff. They brought 180,000 men – more than the entire white population of the ZAR.

Roberts did not make the same mistakes as his predecessors. He attacked not from the south, but from the west – through the Cape, across the Orange River, into the heart of the Free State. He used cavalry to outflank the Boers and cut their supply lines.

On 15 February 1900, British cavalry relieved Kimberley. On 27 February – the anniversary of Majuba – **Piet Cronjé** surrendered with 4,000 men at **Paardeberg**. On 13 March, Roberts occupied Bloemfontein. On 5 June, he entered Pretoria. Kruger fled to Europe, never to return.

The republics were annexed. The flags were lowered. The war seemed to be over.

It was not.

## V. The Third Phase: Guerrilla War (September 1900 – May 1902)

The Boers did not surrender. They scattered into the veld, broke into small commandos, and began a guerrilla war that would last two more years.

The most famous guerrilla leaders emerged: **Christiaan de Wet** in the Orange Free State – a master of evasion, always one step ahead of the British; **Koos de la Rey** in the western Transvaal – a genius of ambush; **Jan Smuts** in the Cape – a young lawyer who led a raid deep into British territory.

The Boers attacked railways, supply convoys, isolated garrisons. They melted into the veld. They fought for their homes, their families, their freedom.

The British were frustrated. They could not catch a enemy that did not stand and fight. So they changed tactics.

## VI. The Scorched Earth

Lord Kitchener decided to destroy the guerrillas by destroying their support. He ordered a **scorched earth** campaign: farmhouses were burned, crops were torched, livestock were slaughtered. Thousands of Boer women and children were left homeless, hungry, and desperate.

By the end of the war, 30,000 Boer farmsteads had been destroyed. The landscape of the republics was a wasteland of ash and ruin.

But Kitchener needed to put the displaced civilians somewhere. He built **concentration camps**.

## VII. The Concentration Camps

The British called them "refugee camps." They were neither – not refuges, not camps in the modern sense. They were enclosures of barbed wire, tents, and corrugated iron, where Boer women and children were held – ostensibly for their own protection, actually to prevent them from supplying the commandos.

The camps were overcrowded, unsanitary, and poorly supplied. Disease spread like fire. Measles, typhoid, and dysentery killed thousands – mostly children.

By the end of the war, 28,000 Boer civilians had died in the camps. Twenty-two thousand of them were children under sixteen.

The British also built camps for Black African civilians – to prevent them from aiding the Boers, or to force them into labour. At least 14,000 Black Africans died. The exact number is unknown.

The camps became a horror – and a memory that would never fade. For the Afrikaners, they were proof of British cruelty, of imperial hypocrisy, of a war waged not only against men but against women and children, against the *volk* itself.

## VIII. The Bitter End

The commandos fought on, but they were exhausted. Their families were in camps. Their farms were ashes. Their leaders were dead or in exile.

On 31 May 1902, the Boer representatives met the British at **Vereeniging**. They signed a treaty. The republics would become British colonies. The Boers would swear allegiance to King Edward VII. Britain would pay £3 million for reconstruction. And the question of voting rights for Black Africans – the "native franchise" – would not be decided until the colonies had self-government.

The treaty was a surrender. But it was not a humiliation. The Boers had fought the greatest empire in the world for three years – and earned the right to rebuild their own future.

## IX. The Cost

The war had been devastating for all sides.

The British had deployed 450,000 troops. They had lost 22,000 dead, most to disease. They had spent £200 million – an enormous sum.

The Boers had fielded at most 60,000 men. They had lost 7,000 dead in battle, and another 28,000 civilians in the camps. Their republics had been destroyed. Their economy was shattered. Their people were demoralised and scattered.

But they were not broken.

## X. The Legacy

The Second Freedom War became the central trauma of Afrikaner history – the "Great War of Existence" (*Groot Vryheidsoorlog*). It was remembered not as a defeat but as a sacrifice – a suffering that purified the *volk*, a fire that forged the nation.

The concentration camps were commemorated in monuments, poems, and songs. The *bittereinders* – those who had fought to the end – became folk heroes. The "sufferings of the women and children" became a sacred memory, invoked whenever Afrikaner nationalism needed a moral cause.

The war also deepened the Boer's suspicion of British rule – and of any government that was not their own. They had lost their republics, but they had kept their language, their faith, their identity. They would rebuild.

And, within a decade, they would rule.
