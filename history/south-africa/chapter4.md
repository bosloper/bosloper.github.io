# Chapter Four: The Frontier Wars Begin – 1770 to 1795 – The First Clashes

## I. The Land Between

The **Fish River** was not a mighty barrier. It was wide in places, shallow in others, thick with reeds and home to crocodiles. A man could ford it in an hour, his oxen lowing, his wife holding the children close.

But it was a border. To the west lay the Cape Colony – imperfectly governed, sparsely settled, but claimed by the VOC. To the east lay the land of the **Xhosa** – independent, powerful, and unwilling to yield.

The trekboers did not care about borders. They cared about grass, water, and room for their cattle. They had pushed beyond the river's westward banks and found the **Zuurveld** – a broad, rolling plain of sweet grazing, dotted with acacia and thornbush. It was empty of white men. It was not empty of people.

The Xhosa called it home.

## II. The Xhosa: A Portrait of a People

The Xhosa were not "tribesmen" in the simple sense. They were a cluster of chiefdoms, bound by a common language (isiXhosa) and a common royal lineage tracing back to a legendary leader named **Tshawe**. They were also deeply divided – the **Gcaleka** (the senior line) under their paramount chief, the **Rharhabe** (a breakaway branch) under their own chief, and smaller groups like the **Ndlambe** and the **Gqunukhwebe**.

Each chiefdom controlled a territory, a cattle herd, and a militia of young men trained from boyhood in the use of the **assegai** (a light throwing spear) and the **knobkerrie** (a heavy club). They lived in **kraals** (circular homesteads) of beehive-shaped huts, built from saplings and thatch. They grew maize, millet, and pumpkins. They traded with the Portuguese at Delagoa Bay and with the Khoisan of the interior.

They were not primitive. They were organized, sophisticated, and proud. And they had no intention of surrendering their land to a handful of Dutch-speaking herders in canvas wagons.

## III. The First Frontier War: 1779-1781

The first clash is poorly documented – a shadow war of cattle raids and reprisals, forgotten by official histories but seared into the memory of the families who fought it.

It began when a trekboer named **Coenraad de Buys** (a man so notorious that his name would become a legend) crossed the Fish River with a commando to recover stolen cattle. The Xhosa chief **Jalamba** responded with his own warriors. The trekboers, outnumbered, retreated.

Then the VOC sent a regular force – soldiers with uniforms and cannon – to restore order. They drove the Xhosa back across the Fish River and declared the west bank a "neutral ground" where no one – white or black – could graze cattle.

The trekboers ignored the proclamation. They had not crossed the river to obey the VOC. They had crossed it to survive.

## IV. The Second Frontier War: 1789-1793

A decade of uneasy peace followed. Then, in 1789, a second war erupted – a confusing swirl of shifting alliances and opportunistic raiding. The Xhosa chief **Ndlambe**, ambitious and restless, attacked his own Rharhabe rivals. The trekboers, seeing an opening, joined the fight on one side, then the other, then neither.

The VOC, exasperated, tried to enforce a new boundary – the **Sunday's River**, further east than the Fish. But the trekboers had already pushed beyond it. They were now living among the Xhosa, trading with them, fighting them, sometimes intermarrying with them. The frontier had become a zone, not a line.

## V. The Politics of the Frontier

In Amsterdam, the directors of the VOC wrung their hands. They did not want a war. They did not want to pay for soldiers. They wanted profit – and the Cape, for all its strategic value, was a money-losing enterprise.

At the Cape itself, the officials were divided. Some, like Governor **Joachim van Plettenberg**, tried to accommodate the trekboers, granting them land and recognizing their claims. Others, like the fiscal (prosecutor) **Willem Boers**, saw them as a lawless mob that needed to be crushed.

The trekboers, for their part, had learned a bitter lesson: the VOC would not protect them. If they wanted to keep their land, they would have to fight for it themselves. And if they had to fight, they would fight by their own rules – not by the rules of Amsterdam or Cape Town.

## VI. The Kommando as Institution

The system that emerged on the frontier was the **kommando**: a citizen militia, summoned by a *veld-kornet* or *kommandant*, comprising every able-bodied man within a day's ride. They brought their own horses, their own guns, their own supply of coffee and biltong. They elected their own officers. They answered to no one outside the commando.

The kommando was not a disciplined army. It was a posse – angry, impatient, and often brutal. When a trekboer's cattle were stolen, he called his neighbours. They rode out, tracked the raiders, and shot them. If the raiders had fled to a Xhosa village, the commando might burn the village and kill the inhabitants. If children were captured, they were sometimes sold into servitude.

There was no due process. There was no trial. There was only the *wet van die geweer* – the law of the gun.

## VII. The Beginning of the Xhosa Memory

The Xhosa, for their part, remembered every raid, every burnt kraal, every family member killed or carried away. They remembered the trekboers' contempt for their customs, their refusal to recognize their chiefs, their assumption that the land belonged to whoever could hold it.

They did not yet have a word for "Afrikaner." They called the white men *abelungu* – a term that signified "pale ones," "strangers," "demons with clothes." They saw them as greedy, violent, and without respect for the ancestors.

They also saw them as strong. The guns of the trekboers were terrifying. The assegai could not match the musket. The Xhosa knew they would need to adapt – to acquire their own guns, to form their own alliances, to fight smarter, not harder.

The first two wars were skirmishes. The wars to come would be something else entirely.

## VIII. Life on the Edge

Despite the violence, or perhaps because of it, the frontier attracted a certain kind of person – the restless, the reckless, the desperate. Trekboer families grew large, their children raised in the saddle, taught to shoot before they could read. They were lean, sun-browned, and hard. They could ride a hundred miles without resting, track a stolen ox through rocky ground, and sleep beneath the stars in any weather.

Their women were equally tough. They gave birth in wagons, raised children with no doctor nearby, tended wounds, and defended the home when the men were away. They were the *volksmoeders* (mothers of the nation) – a concept that would later be romanticized, but in truth was forged in fire and blood.

They also owned slaves. The frontier economy could not function without unfree labour – Khoisan captives, children stolen in raids, men and women bought from slave traders at the Cape. The trekboers did not see this as hypocritical. They saw it as necessary. The land was vast, the threats were many, and a white man could not do everything himself.

## IX. The VOC's Twilight

By 1795, the VOC was dying. Its debts were staggering, its trade routes were compromised by war with England, and its authority at the Cape was crumbling. The trekboers had effectively seceded from colonial control. The Khoikhoi had been reduced to servitude. The Xhosa were pressing westward, seeking new land for their own growing population.

And then, from across the sea, came the **British**.

The French Revolution had sent shockwaves through Europe. The Dutch Republic fell to French armies. The British, fearing that the Cape would fall to their French enemies, decided to seize it first.

In June 1795, a British fleet appeared off the coast of the Cape. After a brief campaign, the VOC surrendered. The Union Jack flew over the Castle of Good Hope.

The trekboers, far to the east, barely noticed. They had never been ruled by Amsterdam. They would not be ruled by London. They would be ruled, they believed, only by God and themselves.

## X. The End of an Era

The fall of the VOC marked the end of the first phase of South African history. The Company had given the Afrikaner people their start – the land, the slaves, the language, the faith. But the Company had also tried to restrain them, to tax them, to tell them where they could and could not go.

That era was over. The trekboers were now on their own, face to face with the Xhosa on a frontier that stretched for hundreds of miles, with no authority but their own.

The British would try to rule them. They would fail. The Afrikaners would rebel, trek, and fight their way into the interior, carrying their guns, their Bibles, and their simmering anger at any power that tried to fence them in.

But that is a story for the next chapter.
