# Chapter Seventeen: The Apartheid Election – 1948 – The Triumph of the Purified Nation

## I. The Aftermath of War

The world had changed.

The war was over. The Nazi regime was destroyed, its leaders hanged, its ideology discredited. The Holocaust had been exposed – six million Jews murdered in industrial genocide. The British Empire was exhausted, bankrupt, and retreating. India would be independent in 1947. Palestine was in flames. The sun was setting on the Union Jack.

In South Africa, Jan Smuts was a hero of the Allied cause. He had sat with Churchill and Roosevelt. He had helped draft the United Nations Charter. He was respected in London, admired in Washington, celebrated in the world's press.

But at home, he was hated.

The *volk* had not forgotten the war. They had not forgiven the internment camps, the arrests, the suppression of their language and culture. They remembered that Smuts had called them traitors – for defending their identity, for opposing a war they saw as British, not South African.

The war had not united the nation. It had fractured it.

And the fracture was about to become a chasm.

## II. The United Party's Complacency

Smuts was confident. He had led South Africa through the war. He had won the peace. The economy was growing. The mines were producing. The cities were expanding.

He did not see the threat.

The United Party (UP) had been in power since 1934 – fifteen years. It had grown complacent, arrogant, disconnected from the *volk*. Its leaders were old, tired, and out of touch. Its policies were vague and unexciting. Its campaign for the 1948 election was lacklustre, even lazy.

Smuts himself was 78 years old. He had spent most of the war outside South Africa, serving the Empire. He did not know the mood of the *volk*. He did not understand their anger, their shame, their hunger for change.

He thought he would win – easily, as always.

He was wrong.

## III. The National Party's Strategy

D.F. Malan, the leader of the Purified National Party, was also old – 74 years old. But he was not tired. He was not complacent. And he understood the *volk*.

Malan had spent fifteen years building his party – quietly, patiently, relentlessly. He had recruited the young, the idealistic, the angry. He had built a network of newspapers, cultural organisations, and churches – the *Broederbond*, the *Federasie van Afrikaanse Kultuurvereniginge* (FAK), the Dutch Reformed Church. He had created a parallel universe – an Afrikaner universe – within the Union.

His strategy for 1948 was simple: **apartheid**.

The word was not new. It had been used for decades to describe segregation – separate schools, separate churches, separate townships. But Malan gave it new meaning, new urgency, new power. Apartheid was not just segregation. It was a total philosophy – a complete system for ordering society, separating the races, protecting white civilisation.

Malan did not campaign on a republic – that was too radical, too divisive. He did not campaign on anti-British sentiment – the war was over, and the Empire was fading. He campaigned on fear.

The **swart gevaar** – the Black danger. The cities were filling with Black migrants, flooding the townships, crowding the trains, competing for jobs. The *armblanke* – the poor white – was losing ground. The communists were stirring, the trade unions were growing, the Native Representatives Council was demanding change.

Malan offered a solution: apartheid. Keep them separate. Keep them down. Keep them out.

The *volk* listened.

## IV. The Fagan Commission

Smuts, to his credit, had attempted a more liberal approach. In 1946, he appointed a commission, chaired by **Justice Henry Fagan**, to investigate the urbanisation of Black South Africans.

The Fagan Commission reported that segregation was unworkable. Black workers were needed in the cities. They could not be confined to reserves, not if the economy was to grow. The commission recommended the gradual relaxation of influx control, the recognition of Black urban rights, and a more flexible, pragmatic approach to race relations.

The report was sensible, humane, and politically disastrous.

Malan seized on it. The Fagan Commission, he said, was a plot to destroy white South Africa. It would open the cities to a flood of Black workers, Black families, Black voters. It would lead to racial mixing, racial conflict, racial suicide.

The *volk* trembled.

## V. The Sauer Commission

Malan appointed his own commission, chaired by **Dr. Paul Sauer**, to produce a counter-report.

The Sauer Commission called for the opposite of Fagan: total segregation, rigidly enforced. Black South Africans would be citizens of the reserves – the "homelands" – not of the cities. They would be temporary workers, not permanent residents. They would have no political rights, no property rights, no voice in the white Parliament.

The Sauer Report was the blueprint for apartheid – the systematic, legalised, total separation of the races.

The *volk* cheered.

## VI. The Election Campaign

The 1948 election was fought on race.

Smuts tried to talk about the economy, about reconstruction, about South Africa's place in the world. No one listened. The United Party's posters were bland, uncontroversial, forgettable.

Malan talked about the *swart gevaar*. His posters showed Black faces looming over white families. His speeches warned of racial mixing, of communist plots, of the destruction of white civilisation. His slogan was simple: *"Die kaffer op sy plek"* – "The kaffir in his place."

The *volk* did not need to be convinced. They were already afraid. The war had shaken their confidence. The cities had filled with strangers. The old certainties were crumbling.

Malan gave them certainty. He gave them a name for their fear. He gave them a promise: apartheid would save them.

## VII. The Result

On 26 May 1948, the *volk* went to the polls.

The results were a shock. The United Party won more votes – 524,000 to 401,000. But the National Party won more seats – 79 to 71. The electoral system, which favoured rural districts (where Afrikaners lived) over urban districts (where English-speakers lived), had delivered the election to Malan.

The *volk* had not won a majority. But they had won power.

Smuts was defeated – humiliated, stunned, unable to comprehend. He had won the war. He had won the peace. But he had lost the *volk*.

Malan formed a coalition with the **Afrikaner Party** (a small, conservative group of Hertzog's old followers). The coalition had a shaky majority – just 81 seats out of 153. But it was enough.

The National Party was in power.

## VIII. The Morning After

The morning after the election, South Africa woke to a new world.

The *volk* celebrated. They had waited fifty years – since the defeat of the republics, since the Treaty of Vereeniging – for this moment. They had lost the war. They had survived the camps. They had endured the humiliation of British rule. Now, at last, they were the masters.

The English-speaking South Africans were stunned – then frightened. They had taken their dominance for granted. They had assumed that Smuts would always win. They had not believed that the Afrikaners could unite against them.

The Black South Africans were not stunned. They were not frightened. They were simply – and accurately – terrified. They knew what the NP had promised. They had heard the speeches, seen the posters, understood the threat.

Apartheid was coming.

## IX. The Significance of 1948

The 1948 election was a turning point – not just in South African history, but in the history of the twentieth century.

In Europe, the war had discredited racism. The Nazis had been defeated. The Holocaust had been exposed. The world was moving – slowly, painfully – towards decolonisation, civil rights, and racial equality.

In South Africa, the opposite happened. The *volk* had voted for racism – deliberately, consciously, proudly. They had chosen separation over integration, hierarchy over equality, fear over hope.

The world would not understand. The world would condemn. But the *volk* did not care.

They had their own history, their own suffering, their own destiny. The world had not helped them in 1902. The world had not mourned their children in the camps. The world had not celebrated their language, their culture, their faith.

Now, they would build their own world – separate, pure, Afrikaner.

## X. The Fire Burns

Malan did not waste time. Within months of taking office, his government began passing laws that would transform South Africa.

The **Prohibition of Mixed Marriages Act** (1949) outlawed marriage between whites and other races. The **Immorality Act** (1950) extended the ban to sexual relations. The **Population Registration Act** (1950) classified every South African by race – white, Black, Coloured, Indian. The **Group Areas Act** (1950) segregated residential areas. The **Bantu Education Act** (1953) created a separate, inferior education system for Black children. The **Suppression of Communism Act** (1950) banned any organisation that opposed apartheid – including the African National Congress.

The list grew. The walls rose. The *volk* built their fortress.

The fire that had flickered at Vereeniging, that had flared during the rebellion, that had burned through the centenary, that had been stoked by the war, that had consumed the election – that fire now roared.

The *volk* had won.

But the cost – that would be counted in generations.
