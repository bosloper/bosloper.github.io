# Chapter Nine: The First Freedom War – 1880 to 1881 – Majuba and the Restoration

## I. The Annexation That Broke the Peace

For two decades after the Sand River Convention, the ZAR had struggled – bankrupt, divided, and unable to control its own citizens. The government in Pretoria was weak; the commandos were unruly; the treasury was empty.

The British watched with growing alarm. They feared that a failed state on their northern border would attract European intervention – French, German, Portuguese – and block British expansion northward. They also resented the Boers' treatment of the African chiefdoms, particularly the **Pedi** under King **Sekhukhune**, who had been fighting the ZAR for years.

In 1877, the British High Commissioner, **Sir Theophilus Shepstone**, crossed the Vaal River with a small force. He had no authority to annex the ZAR – but he did it anyway. On 12 April 1877, he proclaimed the South African Republic a British colony.

The Boers were stunned. They had not been consulted. They had not been defeated. They had simply been erased.

Most did not resist – not yet. They hoped that diplomacy would restore their republic. They sent delegations to London, petitioned the Queen, begged for justice.

London ignored them.

## II. The Gathering of the Volksraad

For three years, the Boers simmered. The British administration was inefficient and corrupt. Taxes were raised. Officials spoke English and sneered at Dutch. The *uitlander* (foreigner) population grew, and the Boers found themselves strangers in their own land.

In December 1880, the Boers decided they had waited long enough.

On 15 December 1880, thousands of armed burghers gathered at **Paardekraal** (near modern Krugersdorp) for a *volksraad* – a people's council. They elected a triumvirate to lead them: **Paul Kruger**, **Piet Joubert**, and **Marthinus Wessel Pretorius**. They proclaimed the restoration of the South African Republic.

Then they raised the *Vierkleur* – the four-colour flag of the old ZAR – and swore to defend it with their lives.

The First Freedom War had begun.

## III. The Boer Commandos

The Boer army was not an army. It was a militia – a citizen force that could be summoned in days and dispersed just as quickly.

Every white man in the ZAR between the ages of sixteen and sixty was liable for service. He brought his own horse, his own rifle, his own ammunition, his own food. He elected his own officers – from *veldkornet* (field-corporal) to *kommandant* to *Commandant-General*.

The Boers did not fight in lines, like the British. They fought from cover – from rocks, from dongas, from the crests of hills. They were superb marksmen, trained from boyhood to shoot accurately at long range. They knew the veld as they knew their own farms.

They were outnumbered – 7,000 Boers against 20,000 British troops – but they were fighting for their homes, their families, and their republic.

## IV. Bronkhorstspruit (20 December 1880)

The first clash came five days after Paardekraal.

A British column of the 94th Regiment was marching from Lydenburg to Pretoria, unaware that the Republic had been restored. Near Bronkhorstspruit, a Boer commando under **Commandant Frans Joubert** blocked the road.

Joubert demanded that the column surrender. The British commander, **Lieutenant-Colonel Philip Anstruther**, refused.

The Boers opened fire.

Within fifteen minutes, 156 British soldiers were dead or wounded. Anstruther was mortally wounded. The survivors surrendered.

The British were shocked. They had not expected the Boers to fight – or to fight so effectively.

## V. The Sieges

After Bronkhorstspruit, the Boers invested every British garrison in the Transvaal – at Pretoria, Potchefstroom, Lydenburg, Standerton, and Wakkerstroom. The garrisons were surrounded, cut off, and subjected to sniper fire and occasional shelling.

The most famous siege was **Potchefstroom**, held by **Major John Clarke** and 200 men. The Boers, led by **Commandant Piet Cronjé**, refused to let anyone in or out. The garrison ran out of food, ate their horses, and finally surrendered after three months.

The sieges tied down thousands of British troops, preventing them from concentrating against the main Boer forces.

## VI. Laing's Nek (28 January 1881)

The British relief force, under **Major-General Sir George Pomeroy Colley**, was ordered to march from Natal into the Transvaal. The only route was through the Drakensberg mountain pass known as **Laing's Nek**.

The Boers, under **Commandant-General Piet Joubert**, held the heights.

Colley was an experienced soldier, but he underestimated his enemy. He ordered a frontal assault – infantry marching up the slope, straight into the Boers' sights.

The 58th Regiment was cut to pieces. A cavalry charge by the Lancers was repulsed with heavy losses.

Colley retreated, humiliated and furious. He had lost 84 men killed, 113 wounded. The Boers had lost 14 killed, 27 wounded.

## VII. Schuinshoogte (Ingogo River) (8 February 1881)

Colley tried again. This time, he led a column to secure a supply route and was caught in the open near the Ingogo River.

The Boers, under **Commandant Nicolas Smit**, pinned the British down in a fierce five-hour firefight. The British artillery was useless against scattered marksmen hidden in the rocks.

When the battle ended, the British had lost 78 killed and 66 wounded. Colley's force was shattered. He retreated again, leaving his dead on the field.

The Boers had lost 8 killed, 6 wounded.

Colley was desperate for a victory. He would make a fatal error.

## VIII. Majuba Hill (27 February 1881)

On the night of 26 February, Colley led 554 men – mostly from the 92nd Gordon Highlanders – up the slopes of **Majuba Hill**, a flat-topped mountain that overlooked the Boer positions at Laing's Nek.

The climb was hard, but the British reached the summit by dawn. Colley now held the high ground. He could see the Boer laagers spread below him.

He did not order his men to dig in. He did not position his artillery. He did not secure the approaches. He assumed – arrogantly – that the Boers would retreat once they saw the British on the heights.

They did not retreat.

The Boers, led by **Commandant Nicolas Smit**, **Field Cornet Stephanus Roos**, and **Assistant Commandant Joachim Ferreira**, decided to storm the hill. They crawled up the ravines, using dead ground to avoid British fire. They shot from cover, advancing in small groups, covering each other.

The British, exposed on the summit, broke. The final Boer charge was a rout.

Majuba was a disaster for Britain. 92 British soldiers were killed, 134 wounded, 59 captured. General Colley was shot through the head as he tried to rally his men.

The Boers lost 1 man killed and 5 wounded.

## IX. The Peace

Majuba broke the British will to fight. The government of Prime Minister **William Gladstone** had never wanted the war – the annexation was the work of local officials in South Africa, not London. The death of Colley and the humiliation of the British army made the war politically untenable.

On 6 March 1881, an armistice was signed.

In August, the **Pretoria Convention** restored the ZAR's independence – subject to British "suzerainty" (a vague, contested term) and British control over the republic's foreign affairs.

The Boers celebrated. They had won back their freedom. They had humiliated the most powerful empire in the world. They had proved, once again, that God favoured their cause.

## X. The Legacy of Majuba

Majuba cast a long shadow.

For the Boers, it was proof that they could defeat the British – if they fought defensively, if they used the terrain, if they trusted their marksmanship and their God. This belief would embolden them in 1899 – and lead them into a much longer, much costlier war.

For the British, Majuba was a wound that would not heal. They had been beaten by farmers – by *boers* with rusty muskets and home-made ammunition. The humiliation festered. In 1899, a new generation of British generals would march to the Transvaal determined to avenge Majuba.

The peace of 1881 was only a pause.

The guns would speak again.
