# Chapter Fourteen: The Poor White Crisis and the Rise of the NP – 1915 to 1924 – The *Armblanke* and the Pact

## I. The Shame of the *Volk*

By the end of the Great War, the Union of South Africa was wealthy – but not for everyone.

The gold mines were booming. The cities were growing. The ports were busy. But thousands of Afrikaner families lived in mud huts, their children barefoot, their bellies empty. They were the *armblanke* – the "poor whites" – and they were a national disgrace.

The *armblanke* were not new. The war had created many of them: farmers whose lands had been burned, whose cattle had been slaughtered, whose families had been scattered. But peace had not healed them. The drought of the 1910s had withered their crops. The depression of 1921 had killed their hopes.

They drifted to the cities – to Johannesburg, to Pretoria, to Bloemfontein – seeking work. They found none. The mines preferred Black labour – cheaper, more docile, more experienced. The factories preferred English-speakers – literate, trained, connected.

The *armblanke* lived in slums, in shanties, in abandoned buildings. They were despised by the English-speaking middle class – "poor whites" was a term of contempt, a synonym for laziness, ignorance, backwardness. They were pitied by their own people – but pity is not help.

The *volk* was ashamed. The *volk* was angry.

## II. The Diagnosis

The Afrikaans churches, newspapers, and cultural organisations – the *Broederbond*, the *Federasie van Afrikaanse Kultuurvereniginge* (FAK) – studied the problem. They published reports. They held conferences. They prayed.

The diagnosis was clear: the *armblanke* was a victim of history.

The British had destroyed the republics. The British had burned the farms. The British had imposed their language, their laws, their economy. The Afrikaner had been pushed off his land, out of his culture, into the margins of his own country.

But the diagnosis was also self-serving. The *armblanke* had also been victimised by his own stubbornness – his refusal to learn English, his suspicion of education, his contempt for urban life. The churches were reluctant to say this. The newspapers were unwilling to print it. The *volk* preferred to blame the *uitlander*, the Black man, the Jew.

The solution, everyone agreed, was state action. The *armblanke* must be lifted – not by charity, but by policy. He must be given land, given jobs, given dignity.

The question was: who would do it?

## III. The Strike of 1922

The crisis came to a head on the Rand.

The gold mines were the heart of the South African economy. They employed hundreds of thousands of Black workers – and tens of thousands of white workers. The white workers were skilled: miners, engineers, electricians. They were also privileged: they earned five times as much as Black workers, and they were determined to keep it that way.

In 1922, the mine owners tried to cut costs. They proposed replacing some white workers with cheaper Black labour – a "colour bar" relaxation that the white miners saw as a betrayal.

The miners went on strike. The strike turned into a rebellion – the "Rand Revolt" – a violent, desperate uprising of armed white workers marching behind banners that read: *"Workers of the World, Unite and Fight for a White South Africa!"*

The government, led by Jan Smuts, crushed the rebellion with tanks, artillery, and aeroplanes. Two hundred people died. Thousands were arrested.

Smuts had saved the mines. But he had lost the white working class.

The *armblanke* now had a political home – and it was not with Smuts.

## IV. The Pact (1924)

Hertzog saw his opportunity.

His National Party was strong among rural Afrikaners, but weak in the cities. The Labour Party – the party of the white working class – was strong in the cities, but weak in the countryside. Neither could defeat Smuts alone. Together, they might.

In 1924, Hertzog and the Labour leader, **Colonel F.H.P. Creswell**, formed an alliance: the **Pact**. The Pact would fight the election on a platform of white protection – jobs for whites, segregation for Blacks, tariffs for industry, and a republic – eventually.

The election was bitter. Smuts called the Pact a betrayal of the Empire. Hertzog called Smuts a traitor to the *volk*. The English press supported Smuts. The Afrikaans press supported Hertzog.

On polling day, the Pact won – narrowly. Smuts resigned. Hertzog became Prime Minister.

The flame had caught.

## V. The Civilised Labour Policy

Hertzog moved quickly. He did not nationalise the mines. He did not expropriate the capitalists. But he did something more effective: he used the state.

The **Civilised Labour Policy** was simple: the state would employ whites – only whites – in all government jobs. The railways, the harbours, the post office, the public works – all would become white preserves. Black workers would be excluded, or relegated to the lowest, least skilled positions.

The policy was not called "racist." It was called "civilised" – a word that meant, in the language of the time, white. A "civilised" wage was a white wage. A "civilised" standard of living was a white standard. A "civilised" worker was a white worker.

The policy worked – for whites. Thousands of *armblanke* were lifted into stable, secure, state-funded jobs. They became railway clerks, postmen, telegraph operators, road builders. They moved out of the slums, into houses with electricity and running water. Their children went to school, learned English, got better jobs.

The *volk* was being saved – by the state, by the NP, by Hertzog.

## VI. The Poor White as Political Weapon

The *armblanke* was not just a social problem. He was a political weapon.

The NP used the poor white to attack Smuts, to attack the Empire, to attack capitalism. The English-speakers were rich, the NP said, because they had stolen the country. The Blacks were lazy, the NP said, because they had no civilisation. The Afrikaner was poor – but he was pure, faithful, and deserving.

This was the birth of Afrikaner nationalism as a mass movement. It was not a movement of intellectuals or clergymen. It was a movement of the *bywoner*, the *armblanke*, the man who had lost his farm and found his voice.

The NP gave him that voice. The NP told him that he was not a failure – he was a victim. Not a pauper – a hero. Not a beggar – a Afrikaner.

The *volk* believed.

## VII. The Symbols of Nationhood

Hertzog also gave the Afrikaner nation symbols.

He replaced "God Save the King" with "Die Stem" – "The Call" – as the co-national anthem. He created a new national flag (1928) – the *Oranje, Blanje, Blou* – that incorporated the old republican *Vierkleur*, the Union Jack, and the Orange Free State flag. He made Afrikaans an official language (1925) – equal to English, equal to Dutch.

These were not trivial gestures. Language, flag, anthem – these are the clothes of a nation. For the first time since the war, the Afrikaner saw himself reflected in the symbols of the state.

The *volk* was no longer a conquered people. It was a nation – a nation with a government, a flag, and a future.

## VIII. The Limits of the Pact

The Pact government was good for whites – but terrible for Blacks.

Hertzog's segregationist policies were more systematic, more bureaucratic, more "rational" than anything before. He passed the **Native Administration Act** (1927), which made the British monarch, not the South African Parliament, the "supreme chief" of Black South Africans – a legal fiction that stripped Black people of their remaining political rights. He passed the **Immorality Act** (1927), which prohibited sex between whites and Blacks – a law that would be strengthened, copied, and enforced for decades.

The Pact also did little for the poorest whites – the *bywoners* who remained on the land, working for white landlords, living in mud huts. They had voted for Hertzog – but the benefits of the Civilised Labour Policy flowed to the cities, not the countryside.

The *armblanke* was not cured. He was just bandaged.

## IX. The Legacy

By 1924, the shape of twentieth-century South Africa was visible.

The NP was the party of the *volk* – of the rural poor, the white working class, the cultural nationalists. The SAP (later the United Party) was the party of the English-speakers, the mining capitalists, the moderate Afrikaners.

The two streams would not merge. They would compete – sometimes savagely – for the soul of the white nation.

The *armblanke* – the poor white – had been the catalyst. His suffering had given Hertzog his cause. His votes had given Hertzog his victory. His loyalty would sustain the NP for generations.

He was the heart of the *volk* – wounded, proud, and finally, after decades of shame, redeemed.

The flame was burning bright.
