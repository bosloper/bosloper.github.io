# Chapter Thirteen: The Great Split – 1910 to 1914 – Hertzog, Botha, and the Birth of Afrikaner Nationalism

## I. The Fragile Unity

The Union of South Africa was born in hope – the hope that Boer and British, after a generation of war, could live together as one nation under a common flag.

But hope is not cement.

Louis Botha, the first Prime Minister, was a conciliator. He had fought the British with courage and skill. Now he would work with them – not as a servant, but as a partner. He believed that the future of South Africa lay in cooperation between the two white "races," in shared prosperity under the British Crown, in a slow but steady reconciliation.

Jan Smuts, his deputy, agreed. Smuts was a philosopher as well as a soldier. He dreamed of a South Africa that would be a loyal dominion of the British Empire – but a dominion that would one day lead the Empire into a new, more just world order.

But not all Afrikaners agreed.

## II. J.B.M. Hertzog – The Voice of the Volk

James Barry Munnik Hertzog was a different kind of man.

He had fought in the Second Freedom War as a general in the Orange Free State forces. He had seen his republic destroyed, his people impoverished, his language suppressed. He had not forgiven – and he would not forget.

Hertzog believed that Botha and Smuts were traitors to the *volk* – not traitors in the sense of betraying secrets, but traitors in the deeper sense of betraying the Afrikaner soul. By cooperating with the British, by accepting the Union as a dominion, by putting empire before *volk*, Botha was selling out the sacrifices of the war, the suffering of the camps, the blood of the children.

Hertzog became Minister of Native Affairs in Botha's first cabinet. It was a strange position for a man who cared more about Afrikaners than about Africans. He used it to advocate for segregation – separate development, separate territories, separate laws. But his true passion was the *volk* itself.

He demanded that Dutch (and eventually Afrikaans) be given full equality with English. He demanded that Afrikaners be given preference in government jobs. He demanded that South Africa pursue its own interests, not Britain's.

Botha was patient. He tried to accommodate Hertzog. But the rift grew.

## III. The "Two Streams" Speech (1912)

In 1912, Hertzog gave a speech in Smithfield that would become the founding document of Afrikaner nationalism.

He declared that South Africa was not one nation, but two "streams" – English and Afrikaner – that would never merge. Each stream had its own language, its own history, its own destiny. The Afrikaner stream must be preserved, protected, and strengthened – not diluted by "conciliation."

The speech was a bombshell.

British South Africans were outraged. They saw it as sedition, as racial incitement, as a betrayal of the Union's founding ideals. Liberals were horrified. Even many Afrikaners were uncomfortable.

But Hertzog had tapped into something deep. The *armblanke* – the poor white – listened. The *bittereinder* who had lost his farm, his family, his future – he listened. The *bywoner* who now lived in a mud hut and worked for a British landlord – he listened. Hertzog was speaking for them.

## IV. The Expulsion from the Cabinet (1913)

Botha could no longer ignore the poison. In June 1913, he asked Hertzog to resign. Hertzog refused. Botha dissolved the cabinet and formed a new one – without Hertzog.

The break was final.

Hertzog left Pretoria and returned to the Free State. He was no longer a minister. He was no longer a member of the governing party. He was a man with a following, a grievance, and a mission.

He would build a new party – a party for the *volk*.

## V. The National Party is Born (1914)

On 1 July 1914, in Bloemfontein, Hertzog and his followers founded the **National Party** (NP).

The NP's platform was simple and powerful:
- Afrikaner interests first.
- Dutch and Afrikaans equal to English.
- A republic, not a monarchy.
- Segregation as the foundation of native policy.
- State support for poor whites.

The NP was not a liberal party. It was not a socialist party. It was an ethnic party – a party for one "stream" of the South African population, dedicated to its preservation and uplift.

The English-speaking press mocked it. Botha dismissed it. Smuts predicted it would fade.

They were wrong.

## VI. The Shadow of War

In August 1914, the world exploded. Germany invaded Belgium. Britain declared war. As a dominion of the British Empire, South Africa was automatically at war.

Botha and Smuts did not hesitate. They pledged South African support – troops, supplies, and money. They ordered the invasion of **German South West Africa** (modern Namibia), Germany's colonial neighbour.

For most Afrikaners, this was a nightmare. They were being asked to fight for the empire that had burned their farms and imprisoned their families – against a German nation that had supported their cause, that shared their Calvinist faith, that had sent the Kruger telegram.

Hertzog opposed the war. He called for neutrality. He warned that supporting Britain would destroy the fragile unity of the Union.

Botha ignored him.

## VII. The Rebellion (September – December 1914)

The anger boiled over.

A group of former Boer generals – men who had fought alongside Botha and Smuts – refused to serve the Empire. They gathered their commandos and took up arms.

The leaders of the **Rebellion** were legends of the Second Freedom War:
- **Christiaan de Wet** – the "Wizard of the Veld," the master of guerrilla war.
- **Koos de la Rey** – the hero of Magersfontein, the Lion of the West.
- **Christiaan Beyers** – the Commandant-General of the Union Defence Force, who resigned rather than fight for Britain.
- **Manie Maritz** – a colonel who defected to the German side.

They believed they were fighting for the republic – for the old flag, the old cause, the old dream.

Botha was shattered. He had fought beside these men. He had mourned with them. Now he had to hunt them down.

He did not hesitate. He led loyal troops into the field – Afrikaners and English-speakers together – and crushed the rebellion. De Wet was captured. Beyers drowned fleeing across the Vaal. De la Rey was shot – accidentally, tragically – at a police roadblock.

The rebellion lasted three months. Three hundred men died. Hundreds more were imprisoned.

Botha was merciful. He commuted death sentences. He offered amnesty. But the wounds did not heal.

## VIII. The Legacy of the Rebellion

The rebellion was a failure. But it was also a warning.

It showed that a significant portion of the Afrikaner people would never accept British rule – not willingly, not permanently. They might be defeated, but they would not be reconciled.

Hertzog had not joined the rebellion. He had stayed legal, stayed political, stayed in Parliament. But he sympathised with the rebels. He visited them in prison. He demanded their release. He made them into heroes.

The National Party became the political home of the *bittereinder* – the man who had fought to the end, who had lost everything, who would not bow.

Botha and Smuts had won the military victory. But Hertzog was winning the peace.

## IX. The Two Streams

By the end of 1914, the split was complete.

On one side stood **Botha and Smuts** – the South African Party (SAP). They believed in conciliation, in the Empire, in a shared South African identity. Their supporters were English-speakers and moderate Afrikaners – the mining magnates, the civil servants, the urban middle class.

On the other side stood **Hertzog** – the National Party (NP). He believed in the *volk*, in the republic, in a South Africa that was Afrikaner first. His supporters were the rural poor, the *armblanke*, the men who had lost everything and remembered why.

The two streams would not merge. They would flow beside each other – sometimes peacefully, sometimes violently – for the next eighty years.

## X. The War Continues

While the rebellion burned, Botha led a successful campaign into German South West Africa. The territory was captured by July 1915. South Africa would administer it for decades – first as a mandate, then as a reluctant colonial master.

Smuts went to Europe, joined the Imperial War Cabinet, and became one of the most respected statesmen of the British Empire. He was a friend of Churchill, a confidant of the King, a visionary of a new world order.

But at home, the *volk* grumbled. Why were they fighting for the Empire? Why were their sons dying for Britain? Why was Smuts hobnobbing with the men who had burned their farms?

The war deepened the divide. South Africa emerged from the Great War victorious – but fractured.

The flame that had flickered after Vereeniging was now a small, steady fire. It would not go out.
