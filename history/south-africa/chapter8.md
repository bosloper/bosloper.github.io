# Chapter Eight: The Diamond and Gold Revolutions – 1867 to 1886 – The Transformation of the Republics

## I. A Child Playing in the Dust

The story begins with a child.

In 1867, a young boy named **Erasmus Jacobs** found a pretty pebble on the banks of the Orange River, near his father's farm. He showed it to a neighbour, who showed it to a travelling trader, who showed it to a geologist.

The pebble was a diamond. Not a large one – but large enough.

Within months, prospectors were flooding into the region. Within years, the largest diamond mines in the world were operating on the disputed border between the Orange Free State, the ZAR, and the British Cape Colony.

The quiet centuries of the Boer republics were over.

## II. The Diamond Fields: A Scramble for Wealth

The diamond fields were a chaos of claims, disputes, and violence. Prospectors from every corner of the world – British, American, Australian, German – poured into the diggings. They lived in tents, drank raw brandy, and fought over every carat.

The largest diamond mine was **Kimberley**, a vast, open pit that would eventually stretch nearly a thousand feet deep. It was a man-made canyon, scooped out by pick and shovel, dynamite and desperation.

The Orange Free State claimed the diamond fields. The ZAR claimed them. The Griqua chief **Nicholas Waterboer** claimed them. And the British, watching from the Cape, claimed them too.

The dispute was settled in 1871 by an arbitrator, **Robert Keate**, who awarded the diamond fields to the Griquas – who immediately placed themselves under British protection. Britain annexed the territory as **Griqualand West**.

The Orange Free State was outraged. President Brand protested, demanded compensation, and threatened war. But the Free State was small and poor, and Britain was large and rich. Brand eventually accepted a payment of £90,000 – and a bitter lesson: the British would not hesitate to take what they wanted.

## III. The Transformation of the Republics

The discovery of diamonds changed everything – not because of the money, but because of the people.

Before 1867, the Boer republics were almost entirely Dutch-speaking, Calvinist, and agrarian. Outsiders were rare. Outsiders were suspect.

After 1867, tens of thousands of *uitlanders* (foreigners) poured into the region. They spoke English, German, French. They were Jewish, Catholic, atheist. They were miners, merchants, speculators, gamblers, prostitutes, and dreamers.

The Boers looked at these newcomers and saw a threat – not just to their land, but to their identity, their faith, their way of life.

## IV. The Rise of Paul Kruger

One man understood the threat better than any other: **Paul Kruger**.

Kruger was a Voortrekker's son – born in the Cape, raised in the saddle, hardened by war. He had fought the Ndebele, the Zulu, and the British. He knew the veld, the commando, and the Bible.

In 1883, he was elected President of the ZAR. He was not a young man – he was nearly sixty – but he was strong, stubborn, and utterly unshakeable.

Kruger's vision was simple: keep the ZAR Afrikaner. Keep it independent. Keep it free.

He would spend the next seventeen years fighting to make that vision a reality.

## V. The Discovery of Gold (1886)

If diamonds changed the republics, gold shattered them.

In 1886, a prospector named **George Harrison** discovered gold on a farm called **Langlaagte**, on the Witwatersrand – the "Ridge of White Waters." The gold was not in a river; it was in a vast underground reef, running for miles across the Highveld.

The reef was so rich that a single ton of rock could yield a pound of gold.

The news spread like wildfire. Within months, a shantytown called **Johannesburg** had risen on the veld. Within years, it was the largest city in southern Africa.

The *uitlanders* flooded in – not by the thousands, but by the tens of thousands. They were English, mostly. They were young, ambitious, and uninterested in Boer culture. They wanted gold, nothing else.

## VI. The Uitlander Problem

The *uitlanders* were wealthier than the Boers. They were more numerous than the Boers. And they had no political rights.

Kruger's government refused to grant them citizenship – unless they lived in the ZAR for fourteen years, swore allegiance to the republic, and abandoned their foreign passports. Most *uitlanders* refused. They wanted the benefits of the gold fields without the burden of Boer rule.

Tensions grew. The *uitlanders* complained of high taxes, corrupt officials, and a government that treated them as foreigners in their own home. They demanded reform – the franchise, the right to vote, a say in how their taxes were spent.

Kruger refused. He saw the *uitlanders* as a Trojan horse – an instrument of British imperialism. If he granted them the vote, they would vote for annexation. The republic would be swallowed by the empire.

He was probably right.

## VII. The British Gaze

Britain watched the gold rush with a mixture of greed and alarm.

The greed was obvious: the Witwatersrand was the richest gold field in the world. If Britain controlled it, Britain would be rich.

The alarm was more subtle: Kruger's ZAR was becoming powerful. It had gold, taxes, and the ability to buy modern weapons. It was building a railway to Delagoa Bay (in Portuguese territory), bypassing British ports. It was flirting with Germany, the rising European power.

The British imperialist **Cecil Rhodes** – Prime Minister of the Cape Colony, founder of the De Beers diamond monopoly, and one of the richest men in the world – declared that the ZAR was a threat to British paramountcy in southern Africa.

Something would have to be done.

## VIII. The Jameson Raid (1895)

The "something" was a coup.

Rhodes hatched a plan: an *uitlander* uprising in Johannesburg, supported by a small invasion force from the Cape. The invasion would be led by his friend, **Leander Starr Jameson**.

On 29 December 1895, Jameson crossed the border into the ZAR with 600 armed men. The *uitlander* uprising, however, never happened. The Johannesburg *uitlanders*, many of whom were peaceful miners and businessmen, refused to rise.

Jameson's men were surrounded, outnumbered, and forced to surrender. The raid was a fiasco.

Kruger handed the prisoners over to the British authorities – a gesture of magnanimity that embarrassed Britain deeply. The Kaiser of Germany sent Kruger a telegram of congratulations – a diplomatic insult to Britain that nearly caused a war.

The Jameson Raid was a failure. But it was also a warning.

Kruger now knew – beyond any doubt – that the British would not rest until the ZAR was theirs.

## IX. The Arm Race

After the raid, Kruger accelerated his military preparations. The gold taxes poured into new fortifications, new weapons, new ammunition.

The ZAR placed orders for thousands of **Mauser rifles** (the best in the world), **Maxim machine guns**, and **Krupp field guns** from Germany. They built a new arsenal in Pretoria. They trained their burghers in modern tactics.

Kruger also sought allies. He signed a mutual defence treaty with the Orange Free State. He cultivated ties with Germany, France, and the Netherlands. He hoped to create a "African Concert" of European powers that would restrain British aggression.

It was a forlorn hope. But it was all he had.

## X. The Gathering Storm

By 1896, the situation was explosive. The *uitlander* population of the ZAR now outnumbered the Boers. Johannesburg was a British city on Boer soil. The British government, now led by the ambitious Colonial Secretary **Joseph Chamberlain**, demanded full citizenship for the *uitlanders*.

Kruger offered compromise – a seven-year residency requirement, reduced to five, but no direct representation. Chamberlain rejected it.

The British began moving troops to the borders of the ZAR – to Natal, to the Cape, to Bechuanaland. Kruger saw the troop movements and prepared for war.

He did not want war. He knew the ZAR could not defeat the British Empire in a long conflict. But he believed – he had to believe – that the Boers could win a short one. They had done it before, at Majuba in 1881.

Perhaps, with God's help, they could do it again.

The ultimatum would come in October 1899. But that is a story for the next chapter.
