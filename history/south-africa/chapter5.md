# Chapter Five: The British Interregnum – 1795 to 1814  
*The First British Occupation and Its Aftermath*

## I. The Flag Changes

The trekboers, far to the east, barely noticed. They had never been ruled by Amsterdam, not really. They would not be ruled by London.

But the Cape was no longer Dutch.

In June 1795, a British fleet appeared off the coast of the Cape. The Netherlands had fallen to revolutionary France, and the British – fearful that the Cape would become a French base – moved to seize it first. After a brief, half-hearted resistance by the VOC, the Union Jack flew over the Castle of Good Hope.

For the next eight years, the Cape would be British. Then Dutch again. Then British once more. The treaty of Amiens (1803) returned the colony to the Batavian Republic (a French puppet state). But when war resumed in 1806, the British returned – this time for good.

The trekboers watched from a distance. They did not cheer. They did not mourn. They waited.

## II. The First British Occupation (1795-1803)

The British came as administrators, not settlers. Their first priority was military: secure the Cape against French attack. Their second was profit: keep the VOC's trade routes running.

They made few changes to the day-to-day life of the colony. The laws remained Dutch. The church remained Dutch Reformed. The slaves remained slaves. The Khoikhoi remained oppressed.

But the British brought one thing the Dutch had never managed: **efficiency**. They streamlined the bureaucracy, improved the courts, and began to assert control over the frontier – the very frontier the trekboers had claimed as their own.

For the first time, the trekboers felt the weight of a distant government that actually intended to govern.

## III. The Batavian Interlude (1803-1806)

The Peace of Amiens gave the Cape back to the Dutch – but not to the VOC. The old Company was bankrupt, corrupt, and disgraced. Its replacement was the **Batavian Republic**, a modernising state inspired by the French Revolution.

The Batavian governor, **Jan Willem Janssens**, was a decent man. He tried to reform the colony: he improved education, granted limited rights to the Khoikhoi, and attempted to restrain the worst abuses of the frontier commandos.

The trekboers hated him.

They had not asked for reform. They had not asked for Khoikhoi rights. They had asked for nothing except to be left alone. Janssens represented everything they feared: a government that meddled, that taxed, that cared more for the *heiden* than for the *volk*.

But Janssens would not last. In 1806, the British returned.

## IV. The Second British Occupation (1806)

The Battle of Blaauwberg (8 January 1806) was brief and one-sided. British troops landed at Melkbosstrand, marched on Cape Town, and forced Janssens to surrender. The Cape was British again – permanently, as it would turn out.

The new British governor, **Sir David Baird**, moved quickly. He abolished the VOC's old monopolies. He opened trade to all British ships. He began to impose English common law alongside Roman-Dutch law. He made English the language of official business.

The trekboers did not like this, but they did not rebel. Not yet. They were still recovering from a series of droughts, their herds were thin, and the Xhosa were pressing from the east. Survival was more urgent than politics.

## V. The New Frontier Policy

The British inherited a mess. The Fish River frontier was a no-man's-land of raiding, counter-raiding, and no effective authority. The VOC had tried to keep the trekboers west of the river. The trekboers had ignored them.

The British decided to try something different.

In 1811, Governor **Sir John Cradock** launched a major military campaign to clear the Zuurveld of Xhosa. He succeeded – temporarily. The Xhosa were pushed back across the Fish River. The trekboers were allowed to occupy the empty land.

But Cradock also made it clear: the trekboers were not free to do as they pleased. They would obey British law. They would not raid the Xhosa without authorisation. They would not enslave Khoikhoi children. They would not – and this was the most offensive demand – settle east of the Fish River without permission.

The trekboers listened. They said nothing. They began to plan.

## VI. The 1820 Settlers

The most fateful British decision of this era was not about the trekboers at all. It was about the **1820 Settlers**.

In a desperate attempt to stabilise the frontier, the British government offered free land and passage to British immigrants willing to farm in the Eastern Cape. Nearly 4,000 people – mostly middle-class artisans and labourers, not farmers – accepted. They arrived in 1820 and were settled on the Fish River frontier.

The trekboers watched these pale, soft-handed, English-speaking newcomers with a mixture of contempt and alarm. Contempt because they could not farm. Alarm because they were British – and they were multiplying.

The Eastern Cape, once a no-man's-land of trekboer independence, was becoming English.

## VII. The Seeds of the Great Trek

By 1814, the pattern was set. The British were here to stay. They brought law, language, and a new kind of settler. They also brought missionaries – men like **Dr. John Philip** of the London Missionary Society – who spoke openly of Khoikhoi rights, of justice for slaves, of equality before God.

The trekboers did not want equality. They did not want justice for the Khoikhoi. They wanted land, labour, and freedom from interference.

They began to talk – quietly at first, then with growing urgency – of leaving. Of going east, beyond the Fish River. Of going north, beyond the Orange River. Of finding a place where no British flag flew and no British judge sat.

The Great Trek was still two decades away. But the dream of it was already being born – in dusty farmhouses, around smoky campfires, in the hearts of men who had crossed one river too many and found, on the other side, not freedom, but another fence.

## VIII. The End of the Beginning

The British Interregnum ended not with a bang, but with a census. By 1814, the Cape Colony had 50,000 inhabitants: 25,000 white, 25,000 slaves, and an unknown number of Khoikhoi and free Blacks. The trekboers were a minority, pushed to the margins, their way of life under threat.

They would not forget. They would not forgive. And when the moment came, they would go.

The road east was calling.
