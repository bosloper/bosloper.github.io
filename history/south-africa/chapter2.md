# Chapter Two: The Company's Garden – 1652 to 1700 – The Birth of the Settlement

## I. A Garden, Not a Colony

Jan van Riebeeck did not intend to found a nation. He intended to grow vegetables.

His instructions from the **Dutch East India Company (VOC)** were precise: build a fort, plant a garden, trade with the Khoikhoi for cattle, and above all, keep costs low. The Cape was a *tavern* – a place for sailors to rest and resupply before the long haul to Java. Nothing more.

Van Riebeeck was a surgeon by training, a trader by instinct, and a bureaucrat by necessity. He was also a realist. He knew that the Khoikhoi were unwilling to be his gardeners. They had their own herds to tend, their own rhythms to follow. They would trade cattle for copper and beads, but they would not labour for wages.

So van Riebeeck did what every colonial administrator would do in his place: he begged the Company for slaves.

The first slaves arrived in 1658 – from Angola, from Guinea, from Madagascar, from Bengal. They were the true founders of the Cape's agricultural wealth. They cleared the brush, dug the furrows, planted the wheat and barley, pruned the vines. Without them, the refreshment station would have starved.

But that is a story for later.

## II. The First Free Burghers

The Company's employees were not happy. They had signed contracts for low wages, long hours, and no freedom. They watched the Khoikhoi living as they pleased and grew restless.

Van Riebeeck saw a solution. In 1657, he released nine Company servants from their contracts and granted them land along the **Liesbeeck River**. They would become **vryburgers** – free citizens – allowed to farm for their own profit, provided they sold their produce to the Company at fixed prices.

It was a small step, but a fateful one. For the first time, Europeans owned land in Africa – not as a trading post, but as a home. They built houses of clay and thatch. They married (sometimes with great difficulty; there were few Dutch women). They had children. They gave their farms names like *Groeneveld* and *Rustenburg* and *Welgelegen*.

And they needed labour. The vryburgers could not farm alone. The Khoikhoi would not work for them. So they too turned to the slave market.

By 1700, the Cape's population was a patchwork: a few hundred Dutch, a growing number of slaves, a dwindling number of free Khoikhoi. The seeds of the future were already sprouting.

## III. The Khoikhoi Disaster

At first, the Khoikhoi did not see the settlement as a threat. They traded cattle for copper and iron, sometimes willingly, sometimes under pressure from VOC officials who threatened to withhold trade goods. They watched the Dutch build their fort and plant their gardens with a kind of wary curiosity.

But the Dutch did not stay behind their walls. Their cattle wandered onto Khoikhoi pastures. Their farmers fenced off water sources that had been shared for generations. And when the Khoikhoi complained, the Dutch called them thieves and sent armed commandos to recover the livestock.

The inevitable explosion came in 1659, when a Khoikhoi chief named **Doman** – who had learned Dutch in van Riebeeck's own house – led a raid on the settlers' farms. The war was short but brutal. The Dutch had muskets; the Khoikhoi had spears. The outcome was never in doubt.

Worse than war, however, was disease. Smallpox arrived with a Dutch ship in 1713. The Khoikhoi had no immunity. Entire clans were wiped out. Survivors drifted into the colony, offering themselves as servants, shepherds, labourers. Their independent life as herders on the Cape peninsula was over.

By 1720, the Khoikhoi as a free, pastoral society had effectively ceased to exist in the southwestern Cape. They had become *inboekselings* – indentured workers – or fled across the mountains to join the San in the interior.

## IV. The Castle of Good Hope

The first fort was a pathetic thing: a mud-walled square with a thatched roof. It caught fire, it washed away, it rotted. Van Riebeeck's successor, **Simon van der Stel**, decided to build something worthy of a Company that ruled the eastern seas.

Between 1666 and 1679, slaves and soldiers and Khoikhoi prisoners hauled stone from the quarry at Signal Hill. They built a pentagonal fortress, its walls thick enough to withstand cannon fire, its dungeons deep enough to hold the rebellious and the unlucky. They called it the **Castle of Good Hope**.

It still stands today – the oldest surviving colonial building in South Africa. Walk through its gates and you walk through time. The old VOC emblem is carved into the stonework. The slave bell still hangs in the courtyard. The cells where prisoners awaited the noose are still dark.

For the vryburgers, the Castle was a symbol of authority – of the Company's power to tax, control, and punish. For the slaves, it was a place of terror. For the Khoikhoi, it was a fortress that could never be their own.

## V. The Drakenstein and the French Connection

In 1685, Simon van der Stel began to grant land beyond the Cape peninsula. He pushed eastward, across the sandy flats, into the fertile valleys of the **Drakenstein** and the **Stellenbosch**. He laid out farms, built a village, planted oaks that still shade its streets.

And then, in 1688, a small miracle: the **French Huguenots** arrived.

They were refugees, fleeing the Catholic king Louis XIV. They were skilled farmers, vine-dressers, artisans. They were also Calvinists, like the Dutch, and the VOC welcomed them.

The Huguenots settled in the valley that still bears their name: **Franschhoek** – the French Corner. They brought with them their wine-making skills, their language, their names (Du Plessis, Joubert, Viljoen, Malan). Within two generations, they had been absorbed into the Dutch community. Their children spoke Dutch. Their churches became Dutch Reformed. Their identity melted into the emerging *Afrikaner*.

But their blood stayed. And their vineyards thrived.

## VI. The Trekboer Emerges

By 1700, the best land near the Cape was already taken. The vryburgers who arrived later had to move further afield – north into the **Swartland**, east into the **Olifants River** valley, even beyond the mountains into the **Klein Karoo**.

These frontier farmers were a different breed. They were not settled burghers with neat vineyards and slave quarters. They were **trekboers** – migrating farmers – who moved with their cattle and sheep, living in wagons, sleeping under canvas, raising families in the most isolated valleys on earth.

They learned the veld. They learned to track game, to find water in dry riverbeds, to read the weather in the clouds. They adopted the Khoikhoi *braai* (roasting meat over coals) and the *kaross* (a cloak of stitched animal skins). They began to speak a language that was not quite Dutch – a kitchen language, a *kombuistaal* – borrowing words from Malay, from French, from Khoikhoi, from Portuguese.

That language would one day be called **Afrikaans**.

## VII. The First Church and the First School

The Dutch Reformed Church was the spiritual heart of the settlement. For decades, there was no preacher, only *sieketroosters* – "comforters of the sick" – who read scripture, led prayers, and buried the dead.

The first full-time minister, **Joan van Arckel**, arrived in 1665. He held services in the Castle, in a room that also served as a court and a meeting hall. The congregation sat on wooden benches, the women on one side, the men on the other, the slaves (if they were allowed) at the back.

There was also a school – a small, simple affair where children learned to read the Bible and write their names. But few vryburgers saw the need for formal education. A farmer's son needed to plow, not parse Latin.

Still, the seed was planted. The Church and the School would become, in later centuries, the twin pillars of Afrikaner identity – the only institutions that survived when every other form of authority collapsed.

## VIII. Sport, Wine, and the First Social Life

Life on the frontier was harsh, but the burghers knew how to celebrate. When the harvest was in, they gathered for **target shooting** competitions – a practical skill disguised as a game. They held **horse races** on the flats near Green Point. They drank wine – the sweet, heavy wine of Constantia, famous even in Europe – and they told stories.

The stories were the real treasure. Around the fire, the older men spoke of the Great Flood, of the wars against the Khoikhoi, of the time a lion carried off a slave and how a brave *veldwagter* (field guard) had tracked it to its lair. The children listened, learning their history without ever opening a book.

And they danced. The *rieldans*, the *langarm* – slow, stately dances brought from Europe, but soon adapted to the rhythms of the new land.

This was the beginning of culture: not in concert halls or on printed pages, but in the dust of farmyards, the heat of cooking fires, the murmur of evening prayers.

## IX. The Shadow Beneath the Light

We cannot tell this story honestly without acknowledging the shadow.

From 1658 onward, slavery was woven into every thread of Cape society. The vryburgers owned slaves. The Company owned slaves. The Church accepted slavery as natural – a curse upon the descendants of Ham, a divine permission to possess and command.

Slaves built the Castle. Slaves planted the vineyards. Slaves cooked the food, changed the diapers, nursed the sick. They were bought and sold, branded and whipped, separated from their families.

Some ran away. Some joined the Khoikhoi in the mountains. Some fought back, and were broken on the wheel or hanged in public. Most endured, passing down their grief in songs that would one day evolve into the **Cape Malay** music, the *ghoema* beat, the *klopse* carnival.

They left no written records. But their blood flows in the veins of millions of Coloured South Africans today.

The freedom of the vryburger was built on the unfreedom of others. That is a fact. It cannot be erased, and it should not be forgotten.

## X. The End of the Beginning

By 1700, the Cape settlement had changed. What began as a garden had become a society – ragged, divided, quarrelsome, but unmistakably alive.

The Khoikhoi had lost their independence.
The slaves had lost their liberty.
The vryburgers had gained land, wealth, and a growing restlessness.
And in the distant interior, beyond the mountains, beyond the knowledge of any official in the Castle, the first trekboers were already pushing eastward, toward a river called the **Fish**, and a frontier that would not be peaceful.

The nursery of the Afrikaner was now ready. The growing pain was about to begin.
