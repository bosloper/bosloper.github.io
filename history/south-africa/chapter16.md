# Chapter Sixteen: World War II and the Ossewa-Brandwag – 1939 to 1945 – The Fracture of the *Volk*

## I. The Vote That Split the Nation

On 1 September 1939, Germany invaded Poland. Two days later, Britain and France declared war. As a sovereign dominion within the British Commonwealth, South Africa was not automatically at war. The decision was Parliament's.

On 4 September 1939, the House of Assembly gathered in Cape Town. The debate was electric. Hertzog, now old and frail, argued for neutrality. South Africa had no quarrel with Germany, he said. The Great War had cost the *volk* too much. Let Europe fight its own battles.

Smuts, his deputy, argued for war. South Africa was a member of the Commonwealth, he said. Britain was fighting for civilisation, for democracy, for the rule of law. If Germany won, the world would be dark for a generation.

The vote was 80 to 67 – Smuts won. Hertzog resigned. Smuts became Prime Minister.

The *volk* was split. The war was not a foreign conflict. For many Afrikaners, it was a continuation of the Freedom War – the Empire's war, fought for the Empire's interests, against a German people who had supported the Boer cause.

The flame of Afrikaner nationalism flickered – and then flared.

## II. The Ossewa-Brandwag (OB) – The Ox-Wagon Sentinel

The centenary of the Great Trek had awakened something deep in the Afrikaner soul. The ox-wagon – the *Ossewa* – had become a symbol of the *volk* on the move, the *volk* choosing its own destiny.

But movement requires protection. In February 1939 – just months before the war – a new organisation was founded in Bloemfontein. It was called the **Ossewa-Brandwag** (OB) – the Ox-wagon Sentinel, or Ox-wagon Fireguard.

The OB was not a political party. It was a *volk* movement – a cultural, paramilitary, quasi-religious brotherhood dedicated to the survival and triumph of the Afrikaner people. Its leader was **Colonel J.C. "Hans" van Rensburg**, a charismatic, ambitious man who had served as a administrator of the Orange Free State and had visited Nazi Germany in the 1930s. He was deeply impressed by German discipline, order, and national pride.

The OB's structure mimicked that of the Nazi Party. It had uniforms, ranks, salutes, and a Führer principle – absolute loyalty to Van Rensburg as *Kommandant-Generaal*. It had a paramilitary wing, the **Stormjaers** (Storm Troopers), trained for combat. It had a women's wing, a youth wing, a cultural wing.

The OB's ideology was simple: a Christian-National Afrikaner republic, free from British influence, free from Jewish capitalism, free from Black domination. It was anti-British, anti-Semitic, anti-communist, and anti-parliamentary. It rejected Smuts's government as illegitimate. It rejected the war as a British conspiracy.

At its peak in 1941, the OB claimed 400,000 members – nearly a quarter of the Afrikaner population.

## III. The *Stormjaers* – The Storm Troopers

The most radical element of the OB was the **Stormjaers**.

They were the shock troops, the vanguard, the true believers. They wore grey uniforms with a flaming torch emblem. They trained in secret camps. They swore oaths of blood.

The Stormjaers did not just talk about revolution. They prepared for it. They stockpiled weapons. They conducted military exercises. They planned sabotage.

Their slogan was *"Liever dood dan slaaf"* – "Rather dead than a slave." It was the cry of the *bittereinder*, resurrected for a new generation.

## IV. The Sabotage Campaign

From 1941 to 1944, the Stormjaers waged a low-level war against the Smuts government.

They blew up railway lines, cutting the supply routes to the North African front. They sabotaged power stations, plunging Johannesburg into darkness. They bombed post offices, police stations, and military depots. They robbed banks to fund their operations.

The most dramatic act of sabotage was the **Lichtenburg bank robbery** of 1942, in which a group of Stormjaers disguised as policemen stole £22,000 in gold and cash. The money was never recovered.

The government responded with arrests, detentions, and internment. By 1943, more than 2,000 OB members were held in camps without trial.

But the sabotage continued. The *volk* was at war with itself.

## V. The Internment Camps

The Smuts government created special camps to hold suspected OB members and other pro-Nazi Afrikaners. The largest was at **Koffiefontein** in the Free State, followed by **Baviaanspoort** near Pretoria and **Knysna** on the Garden Route.

The camps were not concentration camps – not like the British camps of 1900-1902. Food was adequate. Medical care was provided. Families were not separated. But they were prisons, nonetheless, and the men inside were not criminals – they were political prisoners, detained without charge, without trial, without recourse.

The OB called them "concentration camps" – deliberately invoking the memory of the Second Freedom War. The comparison was exaggerated, but it was effective. The camps became a rallying cry for the nationalist cause.

## VI. The Axis Sympathies

Many OB members openly supported Nazi Germany. They saw Hitler as a liberator – a man who had broken the chains of Versailles, who had restored German pride, who was fighting the same British Empire that had destroyed the republics.

Van Rensburg was careful – he never openly called for a Nazi victory – but his sympathies were clear. He visited Germany in 1936 and 1939. He corresponded with Nazi officials. He received funding from German intelligence.

Some OB members went further. After the war, it was discovered that a network of OB sympathisers had been prepared to assist a German invasion of South Africa – an invasion that never came, but that the OB welcomed.

The Smuts government was not wrong to see the OB as a fifth column. The question was whether the threat was real – or exaggerated to suppress Afrikaner nationalism.

The *volk* believed the latter.

## VII. The Anti-Semitic Turn

The war also intensified anti-Semitism among hardline Afrikaner nationalists.

South Africa had a Jewish population of about 90,000 – mostly refugees from Lithuania, Latvia, and other parts of Eastern Europe. Many were active in business, the professions, and the left-wing politics that Smuts supported.

The OB and the Purified National Party blamed the Jews for the war, for capitalism, for communism, for everything that threatened the *volk*. They spread conspiracy theories about Jewish control of the press, the banks, the government. They demanded restrictions on Jewish immigration – and got them, as early as 1937, with the **Aliens Act**, which effectively closed South Africa's doors to Jewish refugees.

Malan himself was not overtly anti-Semitic – he was a Calvinist theologian, not a Nazi – but he did nothing to restrain the anti-Semitism of his followers. The *volk* was being taught to hate.

## VIII. The Battle for the *Volk*

The war was not just between the Allies and the Axis. It was between two visions of Afrikanerdom.

On one side stood Smuts and the United Party. They believed that the Afrikaner future lay within the British Commonwealth – that South Africa could be both independent and loyal, both Afrikaner and British. They fought for the Empire, and they expected the *volk* to follow.

On the other side stood Malan and the Purified National Party – and, more radically, Van Rensburg and the OB. They believed that the Afrikaner future lay outside the Empire – in a republic, pure and independent. They saw the war as a British plot, Smuts as a traitor, and the Empire as the enemy.

For four years, this battle raged – in Parliament, in the press, in the streets. The *volk* was divided. Families were split. Churches were torn. Friends became enemies.

By 1943, Malan had won the political battle. He had persuaded most Afrikaners that the OB's radicalism was reckless – that sabotage, paramilitarism, and Nazi sympathies would discredit the nationalist cause. Van Rensburg's influence waned. The OB declined.

But the OB had done its work. It had radicalised a generation. It had shown that the *volk* was willing to fight – not just against the British, but against the *volk* itself.

## IX. The War's End

By 1945, the war in Europe was over. Germany was defeated. The Nazi regime was destroyed.

Smuts returned from Europe a hero. He had helped draft the United Nations Charter. He had been a friend and adviser to Churchill. He was respected around the world.

But at home, he was hated.

The *volk* had not forgotten. They remembered the internment camps, the arrests, the suppression of their culture. They remembered that Smuts had fought Germany – not for South Africa, but for Britain. They remembered that he had called them "Nazis" and "traitors."

The war was over. But the peace would be brief.

The *volk* was waiting.

## X. The Purified Nation

After the war, the OB faded – its leaders discredited, its methods rejected, its moment passed. Van Rensburg retired to his farm. The Stormjaers laid down their weapons.

But the Purified National Party, under Malan, was still there. It had not compromised. It had not collaborated with the Empire. It had not wavered from the goal: a republic, free from British control, ruled by Afrikaners, for Afrikaners.

The *volk* had not forgotten the war. They had not forgotten the camps, the arrests, the insults. They had not forgotten that Smuts had called them traitors – for defending their language, their culture, their identity.

They would remember.

And they would vote.
